TITLE: Domain freedom motivates students but increases lecturer workload
DATE: 08/06/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Lecturer interview 4
RELATED RQ(s): 
RELATED CODES: optional domain choice with default fallback; domain complexity gatekeeping; scoping complex domains with stakeholders; domain freedom increases lecturer workload; unfamiliar domain examples feel unfair; modelling needs sufficient problem complexity
CATEGORY / THEME: Course design / fairness

1. What is happening here?
The lecturer lets students choose project domains when possible, grounded in personal motivation and prior experience of disliking imposed domains such as football examples. Domains that are too simple or too complex are moderated through approval and scoping. This freedom creates more work for the lecturer than a single shared assignment would.

2. Why is this analytically important?
It shows a deliberate trade-off between fairness, motivation, authenticity, and teacher capacity. Freedom is not presented as easier or cheaper, but as pedagogically preferable.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
Domain familiarity and interest affect engagement, but unconstrained choice creates heterogeneous projects that are harder to assess and support. The lecturer manages fairness through domain gatekeeping rather than identical briefs.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "Usually I give them freedom... I always provide a problem domain for those who do not have one"
- "If students propose something too simple, I explain that it is not enough"
- "Then we do scoping and pruning"
- "Giving them that freedom creates much more work for me"
- "I did not like football... I felt disadvantaged"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
Freedom supports motivation, but students under evaluation stress may prefer uniform, prescriptive tasks. The lecturer accepts higher workload to protect motivational and authenticity benefits.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
Compared with lecturer interview 3, who standardised domains partly for fairness and integrity at scale, L4's small advanced class enables the opposite choice. Compared with student interview 1, where authentic domains increased effort, L4 institutionalises that principle through student choice.

7. Implications
Domain freedom may work best in smaller advanced courses with strong feedback loops. Larger courses may need hybrid models: choice within approved bounds or themed variants of a common structure.

8. Follow-up questions
How does the lecturer balance feedback time across heterogeneous domains? Do students with self-chosen domains show better requirements quality?
