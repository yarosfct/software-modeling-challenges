TITLE: Meaning and consistency matter more than notation memorisation
DATE: 08/06/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Lecturer interview 4
RELATED RQ(s): 
RELATED CODES: meaning over notation memorisation; custom legend allowed in exams; comparative model interpretation in assessment; granularity and stopping problem; documented model rationale required; model justification and quality argumentation
CATEGORY / THEME: Assessment / model quality

1. What is happening here?
The lecturer evaluates whether students understand what model elements mean, how alternatives compare, and how models cohere across perspectives. Notation details matter less than semantics; students may use legends in exams. A persistent difficulty is knowing when a model is enough and documenting what is intentionally left out.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This defines model quality in semantic and argumentative terms rather than syntactic perfection. It also reveals a major unresolved teaching challenge: stopping rules and explicit rationale.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
Students can draw symbols but struggle to explain meaning, compare representations, justify choices, and decide adequate granularity. Quality is multidimensional and not reducible to exam recall.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "notation is not the most relevant thing... What I care about is the meaning of the symbol"
- "they can give me a legend"
- "ask which one better represents the meaning"
- "understanding what is enough and what the ideal level of granularity is"
- "They also need to justify why the model is the way it is"
- "students need to know what is being lost and why"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
The lecturer uses heuristics such as Miller's rule, but admits even professionals struggle with abstraction level. Rules help, but judgement remains experiential.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
Compared with lecturer interview 2, where quality was made visible but student self-evaluation remained weak, L4 tries to teach quality through comparison, peer criticism, and rationale documentation. Compared with student interview 3, who used requirement-recovery as a personal quality test, L4's criteria are broader and more argumentative.

7. Implications
Assessment and classroom activities should reward meaning, consistency, and justification more than symbol recall. Explicit exercises on "what is enough" and "what is omitted" may be needed.

8. Follow-up questions
Do students improve rationale quality over project milestones? Which model family makes stopping rules hardest?
