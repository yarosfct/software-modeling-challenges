MEMO ID: S2M3
TITLE: Relationship modelling is learned through examples and analogy
DATE: 23/03/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Student interview 2
RELATED RQ(s): 
RELATED CODES: relationship representation as hardest part; relational nuance hard to capture; arrows insufficient for nuance; learning from completed examples; example-based relation decoding; analogy from example to own case; no explicit step-by-step instruction; implicit procedure copied from teacher
CATEGORY / THEME: Teaching support / scaffolding

1. What is happening here?
The student identifies relationships as the hardest part of modelling and says he learned how to represent them mainly by looking at completed examples and mapping their relations onto his own case.

2. Why is this analytically important?
It supports the idea that students may need procedural and analogical support for modelling, especially when relation semantics are not easily obvious from notation alone.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
The main difficulty is not placing the objects, but deciding what kind of relation a situation calls for and how that nuance can be represented. Without explicit process instruction, students rely on imitation and analogy.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "the hardest part... is how to represent relationships between the objects"
- "there's always a very big... nuance there"
- "may not be able to completely explain through an arrow"
- "I looked at a model that was already done"
- "if this means this, then in my project... I need to use that arrow"
- "he did it in his way and maybe I just copied it"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
The student describes his approach as somewhat structured, but the structure seems to come more from copied example logic than from explicitly taught steps.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
This resonates strongly with the lecturer's emphasis on examples and step-by-step support. It also complements student interview 1, where text-to-model worked examples were explicitly requested.

7. Implications
Worked examples that focus specifically on relation choice and meaning may be especially valuable. The challenge is not only "what symbol is this?" but "why is this relation appropriate here?"

8. Follow-up questions
Would explicit relation-comparison examples help more than full solved models? How often do students mis-model relations even when they identify the main entities correctly?
