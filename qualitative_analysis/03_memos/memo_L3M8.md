TITLE: LLMs are framed as both a disruption risk and the best route to semantic feedback
DATE: 09/04/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Lecturer interview 3
RELATED RQ(s): 
RELATED CODES: LLM semantic matching for constraints; LLM shortcut risk; tool lacks next-step guidance; tutoring layer as desirable add-on; LLMs are disruptive to modelling education; abstraction must remain a trained human capability; LLM-based natural-language explanation of models; semantic feedback gap in current tools
CATEGORY / THEME: LLMs / desired supports

1. What is happening here?
The lecturer sees large language models as deeply disruptive for modelling education, but also as the most promising route to semantic feedback. Rather than generating student answers directly, the preferred role is explaining what a student's model means and comparing that meaning with the original intent.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This is one of the clearest statements in the interviews about how AI could change modelling pedagogy. The lecturer does not want syntax-only support; the real need is semantic explanation and reflective feedback.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
There is a sharp tension between AI as a learning partner and AI as a shortcut. The lecturer wants the former while worrying about the latter.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "compare an OCL constraint with the original natural-language requirement"
- "many of them might simply have asked the model to write the constraint for them"
- "The tool itself does not provide much guidance"
- "large language models are highly disruptive in this area"
- "abstraction has to be trained"
- "the tool would explain back in natural language what that model actually means"
- "What is still missing is semantic checking"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
The lecturer wants AI integrated into modelling education, yet also worries that the same technology could undermine the development of human abstraction skills.

7. Implications
If AI is used in modelling education, a feedback-oriented design may be far more defensible pedagogically than answer-generation tools.

8. Follow-up questions
What safeguards would keep an LLM feedback tool from turning into an answer generator? Would students actually use natural-language explanations to self-correct?
