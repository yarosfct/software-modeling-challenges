TITLE: Whiteboard, peer review, and role-play simulate professional requirements work
DATE: 08/06/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Lecturer interview 4
RELATED RQ(s): 
RELATED CODES: whiteboard-centric teaching; whiteboard peer review across groups; role-play stakeholder interviews in class; small class enables interactive pedagogy; no upfront worked solutions; iterative model revision expected
CATEGORY / THEME: Scaffolding / classroom practice

1. What is happening here?
Because integrated tools are lacking, the lecturer relies heavily on whiteboards, group work, peer review, and role-play interviews. Students work standing up, swap sections to validate each other's models, and simulate client-developer encounters. The lecturer does not solve exercises upfront but questions groups until they revise their own work.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This is a socio-technical modelling pedagogy: students learn requirements and modelling through communication, critique, and iteration, not through polished tool demos or finished solutions.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
Professional requirements work is collaborative, provisional, and conversational. The classroom mirrors that process more effectively than static slides or individual tool exercises, but only at small scale.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "I use the whiteboard a lot"
- "They all address the same problem... Then they swap and validate what others are doing"
- "One team plays the role of stakeholders, while another plays developers or business analysts"
- "If I had a much larger class, it would be much more difficult"
- "No, I do not solve exercises for them"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
Students do not always like the whiteboard approach, and shy students in other interviews wait for teacher demonstrations. This pedagogy demands participation and tolerance for visible trial and error.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
Compared with lecturer interview 2, who described process teaching as explicit but not recipe-like, L4 makes the process physically enacted in the classroom. Compared with student interview 3, who needed professor feedback and complete examples, L4 provides feedback socially and iteratively but not through pre-solved models.

7. Implications
Interactive requirements teaching may require small groups, peer review routines, and role-play structures. Tooling should eventually support, not replace, these collaborative validation practices.

8. Follow-up questions
How do shy students participate in whiteboard and role-play activities? Which parts of this method could scale beyond small classes?
