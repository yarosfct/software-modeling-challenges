# Cross-Case Comparison: Conditional Modelling Value

## Agreed Interpretation

> Students perceive modelling as meaningful when its purpose, audience, and downstream use are visible; when models are disconnected from decisions and implementation, they are experienced as arbitrary notation or extra documentation.

## Value Is Conditional, Not Binary

The student cases do not support a simple division between learners who value modelling and learners who reject it. The same participant may describe modelling as:

- Useful for understanding a complex system
- Effective for communication
- Helpful before implementation
- Necessary for coordination

and also as:

- Arbitrary
- Burdensome
- Framework-heavy
- Extra documentation

These positions vary with the task, audience, domain, tool, group process, and whether the model influences later work.

## Visible Forms of Value

### Cognitive offloading

S1 and S2 describe models as ways to make complex components and relationships easier to hold and reason about. A diagram externalises structure that would otherwise remain mentally demanding.

### Explanation and communication

Models help explain systems to:

- Teammates
- Supervisors
- Thesis readers
- Analysts
- Stakeholders
- People without detailed implementation knowledge

Audience awareness also increases attention to naming, layout, readability, and abstraction.

### Coordination

S4 and S5 show that models can align group understanding and reduce implementation conflict when they are collectively used and maintained.

### Traceability and rationale

Lecturers L2 and L4 frame modelling as a way to connect requirements, goals, quality concerns, design decisions, code, and tests.

### Preparation for change

Lecturer accounts describe abstraction and traceability as supporting system evolution. L4 argues that modelling can make exploring change faster than changing implementation directly.

### Error analysis and validation

S3 uses BPMN to understand process failures. Executable or semantically validated models can expose inconsistencies before implementation.

## Conditions That Make Purpose Visible

Modelling is more likely to be experienced as meaningful when:

- The model answers an explicit question.
- A real or credible audience will use it.
- It supports a project decision.
- It is consulted during implementation.
- Feedback refers to its reasoning and consequences.
- It changes as requirements or understanding evolve.
- Related models or artefacts are traceable.
- The domain feels consequential or relevant.
- The model persists beyond a submission deadline.

Purpose should be demonstrated through use rather than stated only as a theoretical benefit.

## Conditions That Produce "Extra Documentation"

Modelling is more likely to lose legitimacy when:

- Diagrams are produced after implementation.
- The group does not consult them.
- The model is owned by one isolated student.
- Assessment rewards presence rather than usefulness.
- Framework rules dominate the intended message.
- The model has no clear audience.
- Feedback is limited to a grade.
- The diagram is not updated as the system changes.
- Tools make simple representation disproportionately expensive.

In these conditions, even technically correct diagrams can feel disconnected from engineering work.

## Lecturer and Student Emphasis

Lecturers often describe long-term value through:

- Complexity management
- Traceability
- Modularity
- Requirements reasoning
- System evolution
- Professional communication

Students more often identify immediate value through:

- Visual understanding
- Explanation
- Readability
- Team alignment
- Clarifying what to build next

This is not necessarily a contradiction. It suggests that teaching may need to connect strategic claims about modelling to immediate, observable uses within student projects.

## Value and Formality

The data also distinguish formal notation from modelling activity:

- Students sometimes use informal sketches effectively outside coursework.
- Formal frameworks can add semantic precision but also entry cost.
- Self-made representations may feel more intuitive while lacking shared semantics.

The educational question is not whether all useful models must be formal. It is when added formality enables communication, analysis, validation, traceability, or automation that informal sketches cannot provide.

## Value Can Develop Over Time

Some participants recognise modelling's value more clearly in:

- A thesis
- A larger project
- Professional work
- A complex team setting
- A later course with a real audience

Early courses may introduce modelling before students have experienced the complexity that makes abstraction and traceability visibly useful. This supports connecting early modelling instruction to concrete decisions and revisiting it across the curriculum.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Perceived modelling value emerges when learners can observe a model changing understanding, communication, coordination, or subsequent action. When the representation is not used after its production, its formal demands become more salient than its contribution, encouraging the interpretation of modelling as documentation performed for assessment rather than engineering work.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** formal effort must be justified by the task's purpose
- **Learner responses and strategies:** maintaining, consulting, simplifying, or abandoning models
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** demonstrating downstream use and audience-specific quality
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** reducing effort and enabling traceability, validation, or collaboration
- **Social and contextual conditions:** group legitimacy, audience, domain relevance, and professional exposure
- **Assessment and course architecture:** process-integrated models versus final-report requirements
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** cognitive aid, communication tool, coordination infrastructure, or extra documentation

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Distinguish stated usefulness from observed continued use.
- Compare value perceptions before and after professional or thesis experience.
- Examine whether formal notation adds value beyond informal sketching in each case.
- Identify which downstream uses are most visible to students during courses.
- Determine whether model maintenance predicts stronger perceived value.
