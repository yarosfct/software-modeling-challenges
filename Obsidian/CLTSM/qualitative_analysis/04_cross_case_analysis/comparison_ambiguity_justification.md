# Cross-Case Comparison: Ambiguity, Alternatives, and Justification

## Agreed Interpretation

> Ambiguity becomes educationally manageable when learners are given criteria and permission to compare, justify, and revise defensible alternatives; without this, multiple-valid-answer tasks can produce insecurity and authority dependence.

## Ambiguity in Modelling

Modelling uncertainty can arise from:

- Incomplete or vague requirements
- Multiple plausible abstractions
- Different relationship choices
- Unclear model boundaries or granularity
- Competing stakeholder perspectives
- Trade-offs among quality attributes
- Translation between modelling families
- Several models satisfying the same formal constraints

This differs from simple lack of knowledge. A learner may know the notation and still be unable to prove that one representation is uniquely correct.

## Negative Responses to Ambiguity

The cases show several responses when ambiguity is unsupported:

- Waiting for a lecturer to begin or approve the solution
- Searching for a sufficiently similar example
- Choosing the first plausible option
- Changing a defensible model through self-doubt
- Avoiding public presentation
- Treating lecturer expectations as the hidden correct answer
- Adding excessive detail to avoid omission
- Simplifying prematurely to escape complexity

These responses can reduce immediate anxiety while limiting independent judgement.

## Productive Responses

Students also demonstrate strategies for acting under uncertainty:

- Comparing alternatives
- Selecting the simpler defensible option
- Explaining every element in plain language
- Checking correspondence with requirements
- Documenting assumptions
- Recording rejected alternatives
- Using a prototype to test consequences
- Seeking peer critique
- Revising after feedback

S6 provides the clearest example: documenting alternatives and trade-offs made multiple valid iStar decompositions easier to manage and reduced exam anxiety.

## Criteria Make Ambiguity Actionable

Permission to produce alternatives is insufficient without criteria for comparing them. Relevant criteria in the data include:

- Fit to modelling purpose
- Completeness relative to available requirements
- Internal and cross-model consistency
- Traceability
- Readability for the intended audience
- Simplicity without loss of essential meaning
- Appropriate abstraction and granularity
- Ability to explain and defend the representation
- Consequences for later design or implementation

Criteria do not eliminate judgement. They provide a basis for making judgement explicit and discussable.

## Role of Feedback

Feedback can support ambiguity tolerance when it:

- Asks learners to explain their assumptions
- Compares alternatives rather than revealing one answer
- Identifies trade-offs and consequences
- Connects comments to explicit criteria
- Allows revision
- Confirms that a justified alternative can be acceptable

Feedback can reinforce dependence when it:

- Supplies the lecturer's preferred diagram without rationale
- Treats stylistic variation as semantic error
- Arrives only as a grade
- Removes uncertainty before learners attempt a decision

## Role of Assessment

Assessment communicates whether ambiguity is genuinely permitted.

Supportive assessment may reward:

- Rationale
- Assumptions
- Alternative representations
- Trade-off analysis
- Traceability
- Revision after critique

If only the final diagram is graded against an implicit reference solution, students have a rational reason to seek authority confirmation even when instructors verbally acknowledge multiple valid answers.

## Ambiguity Tolerance as Developing Capability

Tolerance for ambiguity should not be treated only as fixed personality. It can develop through:

1. Low-stakes exposure to incomplete problems.
2. Comparison of multiple defensible models.
3. Explicit criteria for evaluation.
4. Practice explaining assumptions and trade-offs.
5. Feedback that calibrates rather than replaces judgement.
6. Opportunities to revise.
7. Gradual increase in problem openness.

Individual confidence and prior experience still influence how much support is needed.

## Tool Implications

Tools can support ambiguity without pretending to resolve it by:

- Recording assumptions and rationale
- Comparing model versions
- Showing alternatives
- Checking formal constraints separately from contextual adequacy
- Prompting consideration of missing perspectives
- Linking decisions to requirements and quality concerns

Tools should distinguish hard errors from warnings, suggestions, and context-dependent choices.

## Boundary Conditions

- Some modelling rules do have objectively invalid forms.
- Not every alternative is equally defensible.
- Excessive openness can overwhelm beginners.
- Justification should not become burdensome documentation for trivial decisions.
- Lecturer expertise remains relevant for calibration.
- Ambiguity should be introduced at a level compatible with learner experience.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Modelling ambiguity becomes learnable when alternatives are made visible and learners can evaluate them using explicit, purpose-sensitive criteria. Rationale, feedback, and revision convert uncertainty from a search for hidden correctness into a process of defensible judgement. Without these supports, students are more likely to avoid commitment or defer to authority.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** multiple alternatives, incomplete information, and uncertain adequacy
- **Learner responses and strategies:** guessing, comparing, documenting, validating, and revising
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** contrastive examples, rationale, and calibrated feedback
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** separation of errors, warnings, alternatives, and rationale
- **Social and contextual conditions:** confidence, prior exposure, psychological safety, and peer trust
- **Assessment and course architecture:** whether alternatives and justification are genuinely rewarded
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** ambiguity tolerance, independent judgement, anxiety, or authority dependence

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Identify which participants distinguish formal errors from defensible alternatives.
- Compare ambiguity in introductory and advanced modelling contexts.
- Examine whether rationale requirements reduce anxiety for all students or add workload.
- Determine how much openness is appropriate at different stages of learning.
- Track whether validation seeking decreases after repeated justified decisions.
