# Cross-Case Comparison: Assessment, Revision, and Evaluation Load

## Agreed Interpretation

> Assessment supports modelling competence when it rewards reasoning, revision, and continued model use, but becomes counterproductive when its density or scale removes the time and feedback needed for those practices.

## Assessment as a Learning Mechanism

Assessment does more than measure modelling competence. It shapes:

- Which modelling activities students prioritise
- Whether students expose and justify their reasoning
- Whether feedback leads to revision
- Whether quality criteria become self-evaluation practices
- Whether models remain active during project work
- Whether ambiguity is explored or avoided

When assessment focuses only on the final diagram, students can satisfy the deliverable without developing or demonstrating the process used to reach it.

## Productive Assessment Features

### Staged submissions

L1 and L2 describe two-stage project structures with feedback before final delivery. Staging creates an opportunity to apply feedback rather than receiving it after the learning task is over.

### Continuous evaluation

L3 reports stronger performance under continuous evaluation than under an exam-only route. Multiple submissions distribute modelling practice over time and provide repeated evidence of competence.

### Revision

Revision allows students to test whether they understood feedback and can improve an existing representation. It also reflects modelling as an iterative activity rather than one-time diagram production.

### Rationale and alternatives

L4 and S6 show that rewarding justification and documented alternatives can reduce anxiety around multiple valid representations and shift attention from notation recall to defensible reasoning.

### Incremental modelling milestones

S5 suggests recurring modelling milestones as a way to keep diagrams integrated with team decisions and implementation rather than leaving them for final documentation.

### Individual accountability within group work

L3 combines group assignments with a substantial individual component. This addresses the need to assess individual competence without discarding collaborative modelling practice.

## Evaluation Overload

Continuous evaluation is not automatically beneficial. L1 and L4 describe a wider programme environment in which students face many simultaneous tests, projects, deadlines, and grades.

Under high evaluation density:

- Students optimise for rapid task completion.
- Attendance and preparation may decline strategically.
- Ambiguous or exploratory tasks feel risky.
- Students request more prescriptive instructions.
- Reflection, iteration, and self-evaluation are compressed.
- Modelling is more likely to be treated as a hurdle rather than a reasoning practice.

The issue is therefore not simply continuous versus final assessment. It is whether the overall workload leaves sufficient time for meaningful cycles of attempt, feedback, and revision.

## Scale and Feedback Capacity

L3 demonstrates the scale problem clearly:

- Large cohorts benefit from frequent practice.
- Broad UML coverage creates many artefacts to assess.
- Manual, detailed feedback is labour-intensive.
- Multiple submissions may exist without revision of the same artefact.

This creates a constrained form of continuous assessment: students practise repeatedly, but may not complete full feedback-revision cycles.

Potential responses include:

- Smaller, strategically selected milestones
- Peer review with explicit criteria
- Automated syntax and limited semantic checks
- Reusable feedback patterns for common problems
- Sampling or focused review of particular modelling decisions
- Revision of fewer models in greater depth

Automation should extend feedback capacity without presenting contextual modelling choices as mechanically correct or incorrect.

## Models as Process Artefacts or Compliance Artefacts

Assessment timing influences how models are used:

### Process artefact

- Created early
- Discussed during decisions
- Revised after feedback
- Maintained alongside implementation
- Used for coordination or explanation

### Compliance artefact

- Created near a deadline
- Optimised for rubric satisfaction
- Weakly connected to group decisions
- Rarely updated after grading
- Detached from implementation

The data suggest that incremental review can encourage process use, but only if milestones are connected to real project decisions rather than repeated documentation requirements.

## Assessment and Quality Judgement

Quality criteria do not automatically become student-owned judgement because they appear in a rubric. Transfer is more likely when students:

- Apply criteria before submission
- Compare alternatives
- Explain why a model is adequate
- Receive feedback tied to explicit criteria
- Revise the same representation
- Reapply the criteria independently in a later task

Grades alone provide weak information about how to improve or transfer judgement.

## AI and Assessment Integrity

Generative AI increases concern about whether submitted diagrams represent student competence. Lecturer cases discuss stronger exam components or protected assessment.

However, assessment focused only on constrained exams may reduce opportunities to evaluate:

- Stakeholder interaction
- Collaborative modelling
- Revision
- Tool-supported practice
- Rationale developed over time

Assessment responses to AI should therefore preserve human-led abstraction, communication, justification, and critique rather than simply moving all modelling into time-limited exams.

## Boundary Conditions

- Not every model requires multiple formal submissions.
- More assessment points can increase workload without increasing learning.
- Peer feedback may be biased or superficial without support.
- Automated checks cover only part of model quality.
- Group assessment may conceal individual competence.
- Individual exams may not capture collaborative or socio-technical modelling skills.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Assessment develops modelling competence when it creates manageable cycles of representation, feedback, justification, and revision. Its effects depend on timing, workload, scale, and alignment with authentic model use. When evaluation becomes too dense or feedback capacity is insufficient, students are pushed toward compliance, rapid closure, and external validation rather than reflective modelling judgement.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** assessment determines which decisions learners must demonstrate
- **Learner responses and strategies:** revision, justification, risk avoidance, and rubric compliance
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** feedback cycles and opportunities to reapply criteria
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** scalable checking and feedback
- **Social and contextual conditions:** workload, attendance, group participation, and access to support
- **Assessment and course architecture:** evaluation structure, density, scale, and integrity
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** maintained process artefact, competence development, or disposable compliance artefact

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Compare courses that revise the same model with courses that submit multiple different models.
- Distinguish the effects of course-level assessment from programme-wide evaluation density.
- Identify whether students experience staged assessment as learning support or repeated grading.
- Examine which feedback mechanisms remain effective at scale.
- Determine how assessment can preserve collaborative and stakeholder-facing competence after generative AI.
