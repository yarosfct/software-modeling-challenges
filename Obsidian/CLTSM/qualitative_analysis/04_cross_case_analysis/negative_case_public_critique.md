# Negative-Case Test: Public Critique and Social Exposure

## Initial Claim

Public critique can support modelling learning by exposing alternatives, surfacing weak rationale, and normalising revision.

## Supporting Case

### S5: Public questioning is productive

S5 willingly presents models and describes public questioning as embarrassing but useful. The teacher's questions expose decisions the student cannot yet justify, and peers help reconstruct the model afterward.

Relevant memo:

- [[../03_memos/memo_S5M2|S5M2: High-comfort students learn through public critique and early sharing]]

This case supports the value of:

- Early draft exposure
- Direct questioning
- Public comparison
- Peer repair after critique
- Repeated participation that reduces anxiety

## Negative Cases

### S3: Trusted peers are safer than public presentation

S3 is willing to share with a trusted peer but avoids presenting uncertain work to the whole class. Confidence grows through course progression, but public exposure remains more difficult than private or peer discussion.

Relevant evidence:

- [[../03_memos/memo_S3M3|S3M3: Quality is checked by recovering requirements]]
- [[comparison_feedback_channels|Public, peer, and private feedback]]

### S4: Private validation supports participation

S4 can begin independently but prefers private lecturer confirmation under ambiguity. Public sharing creates stress, while low-exposure channels make substantive feedback accessible.

Relevant memo:

- [[../03_memos/memo_S4M2|S4M2: Private feedback as affective scaffolding]]

## Refined Interpretation

> Critique supports learning only when its social exposure is tolerable for the learner. Feedback quality and psychological safety matter independently; public visibility is neither inherently beneficial nor harmful.

Public critique is therefore one possible delivery format, not the mechanism that produces learning.

## Distinguishing Three Dimensions

### 1. Feedback quality

Useful critique:

- Refers to modelling criteria
- Asks for rationale
- Distinguishes errors from defensible alternatives
- Explains consequences
- Supports revision

Poor critique remains poor regardless of whether it is public or private.

### 2. Psychological safety

Learners need sufficient confidence that:

- Incomplete models are legitimate discussion objects
- Errors will not produce humiliation
- Alternative interpretations can be defended
- Revision is expected rather than punished

### 3. Social exposure

Exposure can range from:

1. Private self-check
2. Individual lecturer or teaching-assistant feedback
3. Trusted-peer discussion
4. Small-group review
5. Anonymous whole-class comparison
6. Identified public presentation

The same learner may tolerate different exposure levels depending on notation familiarity, confidence, group trust, and assessment stakes.

## Revised Mechanism

```text
Substantive critique
        +
Tolerable social exposure
        +
Psychological safety
        |
        v
Earlier sharing and explanation
        |
        v
Revision and stronger judgement
```

When exposure exceeds tolerance:

```text
High social risk
        |
        v
Withholding uncertain work
        |
        v
Reduced access to critique
        |
        v
Continued uncertainty or private authority dependence
```

## Role of Individual Disposition

Confidence, shyness, and comfort with disagreement influence exposure tolerance. However, these should not be treated as fixed personal deficits.

Participation is also shaped by:

- Classroom trust
- Lecturer behaviour
- Prior public-feedback experiences
- Language and cultural context
- Notation familiarity
- Whether mistakes affect grades
- Availability of alternative channels

## Revised Proposition

> Critique improves modelling judgement when it is substantive, psychologically safe, and delivered at a tolerable level of social exposure. Multiple feedback channels broaden access to critique, while progressive participation can develop communicative confidence without requiring immediate public performance.

## Implications

- Preserve public comparison where it supports visible reasoning.
- Provide private, peer, small-group, or anonymous alternatives.
- Avoid equating public participation with modelling competence.
- Use explicit critique criteria across all channels.
- Allow students to move gradually toward broader explanation.
- Examine whether participation grading creates excessive exposure risk.

## Remaining Tests

- Compare learning outcomes across feedback channels.
- Examine whether learners move toward public participation over time.
- Identify lecturer practices that create or damage psychological safety.
- Test the feasibility of multiple substantive channels in large cohorts.
