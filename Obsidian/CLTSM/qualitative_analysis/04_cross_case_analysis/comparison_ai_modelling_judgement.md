# Cross-Case Comparison: AI and Modelling Judgement

## Agreed Interpretation

> AI should augment modelling judgement by explaining, comparing, and prompting critique, while students remain responsible for abstraction, stakeholder understanding, and justification. Assessment should verify these capabilities rather than merely prohibit AI.

## AI Roles Present in the Data

Student and lecturer cases describe or imagine AI as:

- An explanation tutor
- A source of alternative model ideas
- A plain-language interpreter of model content
- A comparison aid between requirements and models
- A semantic consistency checker
- A feedback layer on top of existing tools
- A shortcut that can generate submitted artefacts
- A challenge to assessment integrity

The evidence does not support treating AI as simply beneficial or harmful. Its educational effect depends on what cognitive responsibility remains with the learner.

## Productive Uses

### Explanation

AI can translate notation or model fragments into plain language, helping learners test whether they understand what a representation states.

### Comparison

AI may compare:

- Requirements and model elements
- Different model versions
- Related models across families
- A learner's rationale and represented decisions

This could support traceability and reveal omissions, subject to verification.

### Alternative generation for critique

S5 treats AI-generated alternatives as material to compare and rewrite rather than as final answers. This use can expose representational choices when learners must evaluate the alternatives explicitly.

### Reflective prompting

AI can ask:

- What assumption supports this relationship?
- What audience is this model for?
- Which requirement is represented here?
- What alternative did you reject?
- Is this level of detail consistent with the model's purpose?

Prompts can externalise evaluation criteria without resolving the decision automatically.

### Scalable formative feedback

Lecturer L3 sees AI as a possible route to semantic matching and natural-language explanation, particularly where manual feedback is constrained by course scale.

## Risks

### Answer substitution

Students may submit generated diagrams without exercising abstraction, selection, or justification.

### Unreliable semantic claims

AI may produce plausible but incorrect interpretations or modelling advice.

### Hidden reasoning

A polished generated model can conceal whether the learner understands the represented decisions.

### Dependency

Continuous automatic suggestion may weaken independent initiation and evaluation.

### Assessment distortion

Courses may move heavily toward exams to prevent AI use, losing authentic project, stakeholder, collaboration, and revision practices.

### False standardisation

Generated outputs may imply one conventional answer where multiple defensible representations exist.

## Human-Led Capabilities

The current data support preserving learner responsibility for:

- Identifying the modelling purpose
- Understanding stakeholder perspectives
- Eliciting and interpreting requirements
- Selecting relevant abstractions
- Managing conflicting goals and quality concerns
- Evaluating contextual adequacy
- Explaining assumptions and trade-offs
- Justifying final choices
- Deciding whether AI feedback is valid

Lecturer L4 particularly emphasises that stakeholder communication, empathy, and trust cannot be reduced to artefact generation.

## Progressive AI Guidance

AI support should follow the broader progressive-guidance principle:

1. Beginners receive explanations, terminology support, and questions about basic consistency.
2. Developing learners compare alternatives and requirements with AI assistance.
3. More experienced learners use AI on demand for critique, traceability, and complex integration.
4. Learners must always review, justify, and take responsibility for adopted suggestions.

Systems should distinguish:

- Formal errors
- Likely inconsistencies
- Suggestions
- Alternative representations
- Uncertain interpretations

## Assessment Implications

Assessment can verify modelling judgement through:

- Oral defence of modelling choices
- Rationale and assumption records
- Comparison of alternatives
- Revision after critique
- Stakeholder or role-play activities
- In-class analysis of unfamiliar models
- Reflection on AI suggestions that were accepted or rejected
- Traceability from requirements to represented decisions

The aim is to make competence visible even when AI is available.

Pure prohibition is limited because:

- AI use may be difficult to establish reliably.
- Professional practice may include AI tools.
- Prohibition does not teach critical evaluation.
- Moving all work to exams can narrow the competence being assessed.

## Tool Design Implications

Educational AI support should:

- Cite the model elements or requirements behind feedback.
- Explain uncertainty and limitations.
- Provide alternatives rather than unexplained final answers.
- Preserve version history and learner rationale.
- Require confirmation before applying changes.
- Support instructor-defined constraints and terminology.
- Make AI involvement visible for reflection and assessment.

## Boundary Conditions

- Some assessments may reasonably restrict AI.
- AI cannot verify stakeholder truth from an incomplete prompt.
- Generated explanations require human checking.
- Oral defence is resource-intensive.
- AI feedback quality will vary by modelling family and representation format.
- Not every learner needs or benefits from continuous AI assistance.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> AI is educationally useful when it makes modelling criteria, alternatives, and inconsistencies available for learner evaluation. It becomes counterproductive when it performs the representational decisions that the course intends students to learn. Assessment should therefore make abstraction, stakeholder interpretation, critique, and justification observable while treating AI use as an object of accountable judgement.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** AI may expose alternatives and inconsistencies or conceal unresolved reasoning
- **Learner responses and strategies:** explanation seeking, comparison, critique, adoption, and rejection
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** scalable prompts and formative explanation
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** semantic checking, model-text comparison, and progressive guidance
- **Social and contextual conditions:** trust, access, confidence, and professional expectations
- **Assessment and course architecture:** integrity, oral defence, rationale, and authentic assessment
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** stronger judgement, dependency, or superficial artefact production

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Separate actual participant AI use from desired future capabilities.
- Compare AI support needs across modelling families.
- Examine whether AI explanations improve independent later performance.
- Identify feasible assessment designs for large cohorts.
- Determine how learners evaluate incorrect or conflicting AI feedback.
