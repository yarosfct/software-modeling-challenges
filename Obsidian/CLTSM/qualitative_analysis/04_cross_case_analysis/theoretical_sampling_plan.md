# Prioritized Theoretical-Sampling Plan

## Purpose

This plan identifies additional participants and data that could materially refine, challenge, or strengthen the provisional axial model.

The aim is not demographic representativeness or a predetermined sample size. Recruitment should target underdeveloped relationships among:

- Representational uncertainty
- Learner-response modes
- Pattern reuse
- Pedagogical and technical support
- Course and social conditions
- Modelling outcomes and perceived value

## Current Dataset

- 4 lecturer interviews
- 6 student interviews
- Multiple modelling contexts, including UML, BPMN, SysML, feature modelling, iStar, NFR modelling, and executable UML
- Strong evidence around examples, quality judgement, tools, domains, feedback, ambiguity, and cross-family integration

Important limitations:

- Student evidence about large courses is limited.
- Longitudinal change is mostly reconstructed retrospectively.
- Model maintenance beyond assessment is supported by few cases.
- Professional modelling practice is unevenly represented.
- Reasons for absence or disengagement are mostly lecturer interpretations.
- Proposed tool supports have not been evaluated as interventions.
- S4-S6 are reconstructed transcripts and require provenance caution.

## Priority Principles

A sampling target receives higher priority when it:

1. Tests several axial propositions at once.
2. Addresses a claim currently based mainly on lecturer interpretation.
3. Adds a negative or contrasting case.
4. Clarifies movement between response modes.
5. Strengthens a weakly evidenced outcome such as maintenance or professional transfer.
6. Can realistically be recruited within the thesis schedule.

## Priority 1: Students in Large Modelling Courses

### Target profile

Students currently taking or recently completing a modelling course with approximately 100 or more students.

Seek variation in:

- Attendance
- Confidence
- Prior modelling exposure
- Use of lecturer, teaching-assistant, peer, forum, and automated feedback

### Why this is the highest priority

This profile tests:

- Whether feedback is genuinely accessible at scale
- Public versus private participation
- Calibration seeking versus paralysing dependence
- Whether optional feedback mainly benefits proactive students
- Whether continuous assessment creates revision or repeated compliance
- Whether absent students can recover from available materials

### Targeted probes

- Describe the last time you were uncertain about a model. What did you do next?
- What feedback was available without explicitly asking for it?
- What happened when you missed a lecture or tutorial?
- Were slides, examples, recordings, or forums sufficient to continue?
- Did you revise the same model after feedback?
- Did large-class conditions change your willingness to share work?
- Which support channel did you actually use, and why?

### Axial relationships tested

- Scale and feedback capacity
- Workload and access to support
- Feedback-channel diversity
- Continuous assessment and revision
- Authority-deference variants

### Suggested initial recruitment

2-3 students from the same large course with contrasting participation patterns.

## Priority 2: Longitudinal or Repeated-Exposure Students

### Target profile

Students who encountered the same modelling family across several courses or completed a modelling course over a long project sequence.

Useful contrasts:

- Early versus late course experience
- First exposure versus repeated exposure
- Same notation across contexts versus several modelling families

### Why this matters

The current model proposes that uncertainty migrates from initiation toward refinement, integration, evaluation, and maintenance. Existing evidence is retrospective and concentrated in S3.

### Targeted probes

- What could you do later that you could not do at the beginning?
- At which point did you stop waiting for examples or approval?
- Which difficulties disappeared, and which replaced them?
- Did prior feedback become a self-checking routine?
- Did familiarity transfer to a new domain or only similar exercises?
- What happened when you moved to a different diagram or modelling family?

### Axial relationships tested

- Stage-dependent uncertainty
- Movement from DA-P to DA-C and self-evaluation
- Reflective versus unreflective pattern reuse
- Prior exposure and transfer
- Development of model-quality judgement

### Suggested initial recruitment

2 students with repeated exposure, ideally one showing strong transfer and one showing notation-specific familiarity only.

## Priority 3: Students Who Maintained Models During Real Projects

### Target profile

Students who used and updated models throughout:

- A thesis
- Capstone project
- Internship
- Long-running team project
- Open-source or professional work

Include at least one case where model maintenance failed or was abandoned.

### Why this matters

The claim that modelling value is socially produced and reinforced through downstream use currently relies strongly on a small number of cases, especially S5.

### Targeted probes

- Who created, consulted, and updated the model?
- What event caused an update?
- What happened when the model and implementation disagreed?
- Did the model influence a real decision?
- Who was responsible for maintenance, and did that person have authority?
- At what point did maintenance stop, if it stopped?
- Was continued maintenance worth the effort?

### Axial relationships tested

- Model legitimacy and shared ownership
- Value and abandonment loops
- Maintenance uncertainty
- Process artefacts versus compliance artefacts
- Tool-supported collaboration and versioning

### Suggested initial recruitment

2 contrasting cases: one maintained living model and one decayed or abandoned model.

## Priority 4: Students Who Missed Classes or Disengaged

### Target profile

Students who missed several modelling sessions, stopped attending, withdrew from participation, or strategically deprioritized modelling.

Recruit without assuming lack of motivation.

### Why this matters

Current explanations of attendance and disengagement come mainly from lecturers. Direct student evidence is needed before making causal claims.

### Targeted probes

- What led you to miss or deprioritize the sessions?
- Which competing obligations mattered?
- Did you consider the live sessions useful?
- What material did you use to recover?
- Which reasoning or examples were absent from the materials?
- Did missing one session create later difficulty?
- What support would have made continuation realistic?

### Axial relationships tested

- Workload and access to support
- Course-material self-sufficiency
- Avoid or abandon modelling
- Lecturer interpretation versus student account
- Programme-level versus course-level workload

### Suggested initial recruitment

2 students with different reasons for absence or disengagement.

### Ethical and recruitment caution

Phrase invitations neutrally. Avoid labelling participants as weak, unmotivated, or disengaged.

## Priority 5: Students Who Reject Modelling Despite Adequate Support

### Target profile

Students who had:

- Worked examples
- Accessible feedback
- Reasonable tools
- Authentic or relevant projects

but still view modelling as low-value or unnecessary.

### Why this matters

This is a strong negative case for the proposed value mechanism. It can reveal:

- Whether implementation identity overrides support
- Whether formalism remains disproportionate to purpose
- Whether professional expectations undermine course legitimacy
- Whether current explanations overstate the power of course design

### Targeted probes

- What support was available, and why did it not change your view?
- Did any model affect a decision or implementation?
- Would informal sketching have achieved the same purpose?
- Is the objection to modelling generally or to formal notation?
- Did industry or project experience reinforce your view?
- What evidence would make modelling worthwhile?

### Axial relationships tested

- Conditional modelling value
- Purpose-formality alignment
- Avoidance despite support
- Code-first identity
- Limits of pedagogical intervention

### Suggested initial recruitment

1-2 information-rich negative cases.

## Priority 6: Early-Career Professionals or Recent Graduates

### Target profile

Recent graduates or professionals with 1-5 years of experience who encountered modelling in university.

Seek variation between:

- Formal modelling use
- Informal sketching only
- No modelling use
- Tool-supported or model-driven practice

### Why this matters

Current professional-practice claims are indirect or based on limited student and lecturer accounts.

### Targeted probes

- Which university modelling practices transferred to work?
- What representations are actually used?
- Who is the audience?
- How formal are the models, and why?
- Are models maintained?
- What makes a model trusted or ignored?
- Did workplace experience change your judgement of university modelling?

### Axial relationships tested

- Formality-purpose continuum
- Professional visibility and perceived value
- Transfer beyond assessment
- Model maintenance
- Lecturer rationale versus actual practice

### Suggested initial recruitment

2 contrasting professionals: one who uses formal models and one who relies mainly on informal representations.

## Priority 7: Cross-Family Integration Cases

### Target profile

Students currently moving between:

- Goals and UML
- NFR and design models
- BPMN and UML
- SysML and UML
- Feature models and system models

### Why this matters

Cross-family integration is analytically important but currently concentrated in L1, L4, and S6.

### Targeted probes

- What information should survive the transition?
- What translation rule did you use?
- Which links were explicit?
- Where did syntax appear correct but meaning become lost?
- Would a traceability table, continuous example, or integrated tool help most?
- Do you see the models as one workflow or separate assignments?

### Axial relationships tested

- Integration-stage uncertainty
- Reflective pattern reuse across families
- Traceability
- Conceptual versus tool fragmentation

### Suggested initial recruitment

1-2 students from a course that explicitly integrates multiple modelling families.

## Not an Interview-Sampling Gap: Tool Effectiveness

Interview data can identify desired tool features and perceived problems, but it cannot establish whether a proposed feature improves learning.

Claims about effectiveness require a small intervention or observational task, for example:

- Compare unguided and guided modelling attempts.
- Observe use of a semantic checker.
- Test a traceability table on a cross-family task.
- Compare complete versus rationale-rich worked examples.
- Ask learners to critique AI-generated alternatives.

If no intervention is feasible, report tool implications as requirements or design propositions rather than validated solutions.

## Recommended Recruitment Sequence

### Wave 1

1. Large-course students
2. Repeated-exposure students
3. Maintained-versus-abandoned project models

These profiles test the largest number of propositions and address important student-evidence gaps.

### Wave 2

Choose based on Wave 1 findings:

- Recruit absent or disengaged students if workload and recoverability remain unclear.
- Recruit modelling rejecters if value appears too uniformly positive.
- Recruit cross-family students if integration categories remain thin.
- Recruit professionals if transfer and maintenance remain central to the final account.

## Interview Design

Use the existing gap-filling student guide selectively. Prioritize one concrete incident and one model or project trajectory.

For each interview, collect:

- Course and notation context
- Modelling stage where uncertainty occurred
- Learner response mode
- Feedback and tool access
- Assessment conditions
- Outcome for the model
- Whether behaviour changed later

Avoid asking participants to confirm the theory directly. Ask for concrete events, decisions, and consequences.

## Optional Artefact Elicitation

Where consent permits, ask participants to walk through:

- An early and revised version of a model
- A model that was maintained or abandoned
- Feedback received on a model
- Two linked models from different families
- A model and the implementation or requirements it represents

Artefacts would strengthen claims about revision, traceability, maintenance, and model-code divergence.

## Sampling Decision Log

For each recruited participant, record:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Sampling target | Which gap prompted recruitment |
| Expected contrast | What existing case or claim it tests |
| Key probes | Questions prioritized for this participant |
| New properties | What the interview added to a category |
| Contradictions | Which propositions were weakened or refined |
| Next decision | Stop, deepen, or recruit another contrast |

## Stopping Criteria

Stop sampling a category when:

- New interviews repeat established properties and relationships.
- Both enabling and limiting conditions are represented.
- At least one negative case has been examined.
- Student and lecturer accounts are sufficiently compared.
- Additional data refine wording but do not alter the explanatory mechanism.

Do not claim saturation for the entire field. Report sufficient category elaboration for the bounded thesis dataset.

## Minimum Practical Extension

If time permits only 4 additional interviews:

1. Large-course student with strong participation
2. Large-course student with missed sessions or low feedback access
3. Student with a maintained living model
4. Student with an abandoned or decayed model

This compact extension would strengthen scale, attendance, feedback, maintenance, and perceived-value claims.

## Preferred Extension

If 6-8 interviews are feasible:

- 2-3 large-course students
- 2 repeated-exposure students
- 2 maintained-versus-abandoned project cases
- 1 modelling rejecter or cross-family integration case selected after interim analysis

Analyze each interview before finalizing the next recruitment decision.
