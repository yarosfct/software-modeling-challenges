# Provisional Core Phenomenon

## Formulation

> Learning modelling involves making and justifying representational decisions under uncertainty. Difficulties emerge when learners lack sufficient cognitive, pedagogical, social, or technical support for those decisions.

## Status

- Accepted as the central organising idea on 2026-06-09.
- Provisional until axial and selective coding test it against all cases.
- Intended to organise the analysis, not force every incident into one category.

## What counts as representational decision-making?

- Deciding how to begin transforming a problem description into a model.
- Selecting which elements of the problem to represent or omit.
- Choosing relationships, abstractions, boundaries, and levels of detail.
- Decomposing a complex problem into manageable parts.
- Translating between modelling families or levels of abstraction.
- Evaluating alternatives when more than one model may be defensible.
- Explaining and justifying why a representation is fit for its purpose.
- Revising and maintaining a model as understanding or implementation changes.

## Why uncertainty matters

Modelling rarely provides the immediate correctness signal associated with compiling or executing code. Learners may face incomplete requirements, unfamiliar domains, ambiguous semantics, several defensible solutions, and unclear quality thresholds. This uncertainty can produce hesitation, dependence on examples or authority, trial and error, excessive detail, premature simplification, or avoidance of modelling.

Uncertainty is not necessarily a defect to eliminate. It is also an inherent part of abstraction and design. The educational issue is whether learners have adequate ways to act, evaluate, and justify decisions despite it.

## Provisional supporting mechanisms

### Cognitive and procedural support

- Prior modelling and domain knowledge
- Strategies for text-to-model translation and decomposition
- Explicit quality and traceability criteria
- Experience moving between modelling families

### Pedagogical support

- Worked and contrastive examples that expose rationale
- Progressive complexity and guided-to-independent practice
- Timely feedback, revision opportunities, and critique
- Explicit support for self-evaluation and justification

### Social support

- Psychological safety when sharing incomplete models
- Peer discussion and shared model ownership
- Group recognition of modelling as legitimate project work
- Access to lecturers, teaching assistants, or trusted peers

### Technical support

- Semantic validation and prevention of invalid operations
- Guidance that explains errors and possible next steps
- Collaboration and model-maintenance facilities
- Traceability and integration across modelling families

## Provisional consequences

When support is insufficient:

- Learners delay starting or wait for teacher initiation.
- Decisions are made by guessing, imitation, or unexamined convention.
- Learners remain dependent on external validation.
- Models become over-detailed, incomplete, inconsistent, or detached from their purpose.
- Models lose legitimacy within groups and decay as implementation proceeds.
- Modelling is experienced as arbitrary notation or extra documentation.

When support is adequate:

- Learners tolerate ambiguity and justify alternatives.
- Models support communication, coordination, traceability, and reasoning.
- Feedback becomes a basis for later self-evaluation.
- Models remain useful beyond assessment and may be maintained as working artefacts.

## Boundaries and negative cases

- Starting difficulty is common but not universal; some students start readily and struggle later with refinement or granularity.
- Notation knowledge still matters, particularly for unfamiliar or semantically rich languages.
- Public critique supports some learners but inhibits others.
- Semantic tools reduce syntactic uncertainty but cannot decide every context-dependent representational choice.
- Authenticity and domain choice can increase engagement while also increasing complexity, inequality, and teaching workload.
- Some uncertainty is productive and should be supported through justification rather than replaced with one prescribed answer.

## Relationship to the research questions

- **RQ1:** Identifies where representational decisions become difficult across interpreting, creating, refining, translating, and evaluating models.
- **RQ2:** Examines strategies and supports that help learners make, test, and justify those decisions.
- **RQ3:** Explains how tools, teaching practices, assessment, domains, group dynamics, workload, and course scale shape the available support and resulting experience.

## Analytic tests still required

- Compare how the phenomenon differs between students and lecturers.
- Test whether it adequately explains cases centred on notation overload or tool friction.
- Distinguish uncertainty caused by missing instruction from uncertainty inherent to modelling.
- Identify conditions that produce independence rather than authority dependence.
- Search systematically for incidents that the formulation does not explain.
- Determine whether learner confidence is a condition, strategy, consequence, or a changing combination of these.
