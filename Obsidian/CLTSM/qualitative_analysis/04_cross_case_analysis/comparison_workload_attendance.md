# Cross-Case Comparison: Workload, Attendance, and Access to Support

## Agreed Interpretation

> Workload and attendance affect access to modelling support, but lecturer explanations should not be treated as sufficient evidence of student motivation or responsibility. The analysis must distinguish observed absence from inferred causes and examine whether course materials are self-sufficient.

## What the Data Support Directly

Lecturers report:

- Declining or uneven attendance
- Students missing concept introductions
- Greater difficulty when learners depend on slides after absence
- Competing assignments and continuous evaluation
- Limited time for practice, feedback, and revision
- Labs being unable to replace missed theory
- High evaluation density reducing space for exploration

Student evidence indicates:

- Partial slides may be insufficient for unfamiliar modelling concepts.
- Complete examples and lecturer or teaching-assistant feedback matter.
- Missing an interactive session can make later independent reconstruction difficult.
- Feedback is sometimes available only when students actively seek it.

Together, these accounts support a relationship between participation and access to modelling-specific explanation, examples, feedback, and guided practice.

## What Requires Caution

Lecturer accounts sometimes infer why students are absent or disengaged:

- Student culture
- Social calendars
- Lack of commitment
- Preference for easier or prescribed work

These explanations are perceptions, not independently established causes. The current student corpus provides limited direct evidence about:

- Why classes were missed
- Whether absence was strategic workload management
- Whether health, employment, commuting, caring, or accessibility factors mattered
- Whether students considered sessions useful
- Whether materials were designed for independent recovery

The thesis should attribute these interpretations explicitly to lecturers and avoid presenting them as settled explanations.

## Attendance as Access to Tacit Modelling Knowledge

The evidence suggests that some modelling knowledge is communicated through live activity rather than static materials:

- Step-by-step interpretation of a case
- Explanation of rejected alternatives
- Whiteboard decomposition
- Responses to student questions
- Peer critique
- Tool demonstrations
- Rationale for relationship or granularity choices

Students who miss these interactions may receive notation definitions without seeing how representational judgement is exercised.

This mechanism is analytically stronger than a simple claim that attendance predicts success.

## Course-Material Self-Sufficiency

Materials can support recovery when they include:

- Complete worked examples
- Intermediate modelling steps
- Explanations of rationale
- Common errors and counterexamples
- Explicit links between theory and lab tasks
- Tool instructions separated from modelling guidance
- Quality criteria and self-check questions
- Recordings or annotated demonstrations where appropriate

Slides containing only definitions or final diagrams may be inadequate for independent learning, especially in unfamiliar modelling families.

Self-sufficient materials do not make attendance irrelevant. They reduce the extent to which one missed session causes cascading difficulty.

## Workload Mechanism

High workload can shape modelling learning through:

1. Reduced attendance or preparation.
2. Less time to explore ambiguous alternatives.
3. Preference for prescribed tasks and visible answers.
4. Reduced revision after feedback.
5. Prioritisation of assessed implementation work.
6. Treating models as final deliverables rather than maintained artefacts.

This is especially significant because modelling competence requires time for interpretation, uncertainty, feedback, and iteration.

## Institutional and Course-Level Responsibility

The analysis should avoid locating the problem only in students. Relevant design questions include:

- Is assessment density coordinated across the programme?
- Are modelling activities given sufficient time relative to their complexity?
- Can missed content be recovered from available resources?
- Are labs designed as application spaces or emergency theory replacement?
- Does assessment reward attendance indirectly by hiding essential reasoning only in live sessions?
- Are support channels accessible to students who cannot participate publicly?

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Attendance matters in modelling education because live sessions often provide tacit process knowledge, feedback, and opportunities to observe representational reasoning. Workload can reduce access to these supports and compress the time required for exploration and revision. However, explanations of absence must remain grounded in participant evidence, and course design determines how severely missed participation affects learning.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** missed reasoning support can intensify starting, decomposition, and quality difficulties
- **Learner responses and strategies:** self-study, seeking examples, requesting help, or avoiding uncertain work
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** live demonstrations, lab support, and recoverable materials
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** recordings, annotated examples, and asynchronous feedback
- **Social and contextual conditions:** attendance, time, access, and competing obligations
- **Assessment and course architecture:** evaluation density, scheduling, and the role assigned to lectures and labs
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** confidence, continuity, disengagement, or dependence on live support

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Ask students directly why they missed modelling sessions, where applicable.
- Compare lecturer assumptions about absence with student accounts.
- Identify which course resources students actually used for recovery.
- Examine whether complete asynchronous materials improve independent continuation.
- Separate programme-wide workload from workload created by the modelling course itself.
