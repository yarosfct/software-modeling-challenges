# Cross-Case Comparison: Worked Examples and Transfer

## Agreed Interpretation

Worked examples support independence when they expose modelling decisions and progressively fade assistance. When they present only a polished final diagram or are reused as rigid templates, they may reinforce dependence on surface patterns.

## Why Examples Matter

Examples appear across lecturer and student cases as one of the most common forms of modelling support. They can reduce the uncertainty involved in:

- Starting from a problem description
- Identifying candidate model elements
- Selecting relationships
- Choosing an appropriate level of abstraction
- Decomposing a large problem
- Applying unfamiliar notation semantics
- Translating between modelling families
- Evaluating alternative representations

Their value is therefore not limited to illustrating notation. They can externalise parts of the modelling process that are otherwise tacit.

## Lecturer Perspective

- L1 uses step-by-step demonstrations, followed by similar independent tasks, counterexamples, and rationale-based feedback.
- L2 combines an explicit process with example-first teaching, while stressing that no example or checklist resolves every situated decision.
- L3 uses examples before synthesis, demonstrations when many students are stuck, and prepared examples for difficult activities.
- L4 generally avoids providing direct solutions in advance and instead uses whiteboard modelling, comparison, peer criticism, and iterative revision.

These cases demonstrate different scaffolding philosophies. All make reasoning visible in some form, but they vary in when learners see a worked solution and how much uncertainty is preserved.

## Student Perspective

- S1 requests text-to-model examples, templates, and gradual increases in complexity.
- S2 reports learning relationship representation by decoding examples and applying analogies to a new case.
- S3 benefits from complete examples and multiple exposures, especially when brief course materials are otherwise insufficient.
- S4 values examples that explain why one relationship or abstraction is preferred and finds contrasting versions particularly transferable.
- S5 uses examples to calibrate granularity and correct an over-modelling tendency.
- S6 uses examples to understand acceptable actor and goal granularity and to connect quality attributes with design decisions.

The student evidence confirms that examples can support several different representational decisions. It also shows that the relevant example structure differs by modelling family and learner difficulty.

## Productive Example Features

Examples are more likely to develop transferable judgement when they include:

- Interpretation of the problem or requirements
- Candidate elements and initial assumptions
- Intermediate or incomplete models
- Alternatives that were considered
- Rejected options and reasons for rejection
- Comparison of too-detailed, too-simple, and fit-for-purpose models
- Explanation of relationship and granularity choices
- Quality checks applied to the resulting model
- Links across representations or modelling families
- A subsequent task that changes important surface details

## Risks

### Surface imitation

Learners may copy visible structures without understanding why they are suitable.

### Example dependence

Students may wait for a sufficiently similar example before beginning unfamiliar work.

### False certainty

A polished solution may imply that modelling has one correct path or answer.

### Weak transfer

Examples may work only when the new task shares the same domain, notation, or superficial structure.

### Suppressed exploration

Showing a solution too early can remove productive uncertainty and reduce opportunities to formulate and defend alternatives.

## Fading and Progression

A provisional progression supported by the data is:

1. Instructor models the reasoning process, not only the final diagram.
2. Learners compare alternatives and identify the criteria behind decisions.
3. Learners complete or critique a partially worked example.
4. Learners attempt a related task with prompts or a checklist.
5. Learners attempt a structurally different task and justify their decisions.
6. Feedback focuses on the reasoning and criteria that can transfer to later work.

The timing and amount of fading should vary with prior exposure, modelling-family complexity, confidence, and task novelty.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Worked examples reduce representational uncertainty by making otherwise tacit modelling decisions observable. They foster independence when learners examine alternatives, apply explicit criteria, practise on varied tasks, and gradually assume responsibility for the decisions. They reinforce dependence when students can reproduce a visible pattern without understanding or testing its rationale.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** examples clarify starting, relation choice, decomposition, granularity, and translation
- **Learner responses and strategies:** analogy, imitation, comparison, and transfer
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** rationale visibility, contrastive cases, fading, and guided practice
- **Social and contextual conditions:** confidence, prior exposure, and tolerance for incomplete work
- **Assessment and course architecture:** whether learners must justify decisions or reproduce expected diagrams
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** transferable judgement, independence, or example dependence

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Compare transfer from same-domain examples with transfer across domains.
- Determine when students prefer examples before attempting a task versus after an initial attempt.
- Examine whether partial examples support independence better than complete solutions.
- Identify variation by modelling family and diagram type.
- Distinguish students' stated preference for examples from evidence that examples improved later performance.
