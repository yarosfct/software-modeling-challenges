# Cross-Case Comparison: Domains, Authenticity, and Fairness

## Agreed Interpretation

> Domain design is a managed trade-off among authenticity, familiarity, motivation, complexity, fairness, and teaching capacity; no single domain policy is optimal across course contexts.

## Distinguishing Key Concepts

### Familiarity

The learner already understands enough of the domain to identify relevant actors, concepts, processes, constraints, or goals.

### Personal relevance

The learner finds the domain interesting, meaningful, or connected to personal experience.

### Authenticity

The task resembles meaningful modelling practice by involving realistic uncertainty, stakeholders, consequences, audiences, or evolving requirements.

### Complexity

The domain contains enough structure to require modelling, but not so much unfamiliar detail that domain learning overwhelms modelling learning.

These properties may overlap, but they are not equivalent. A realistic domain can be unfamiliar, an everyday domain can be artificially simplified, and a personally interesting domain can be unsuitable for fair assessment.

## Domain as Part of Modelling Difficulty

Lecturer L2 treats domain understanding as a prerequisite for modelling and observes that changing domains can undermine transfer from previous examples. The learner may appear unable to apply a notation when the hidden problem is insufficient domain understanding.

Student cases support this mechanism:

- S1 reports greater effort when a domain feels authentic or personally meaningful.
- S4 begins faster and refines more carefully when interested in the domain.
- S5 describes a relatable campus application as making modelling feel real.
- S6 prefers bounded organisational domains that are realistic but still manageable.

Domain knowledge therefore affects what learners can notice, select, relate, and justify before notation is applied.

## Common Domain Strategy

Lecturers L1-L3 describe reasons for using a common or controlled project domain:

- Comparable difficulty across groups
- Fairer grading
- Reusable teaching examples and feedback
- Easier detection of common misconceptions
- Lower lecturer workload
- Reduced copying or recycled-project risk
- Feasibility in large cohorts

Lecturer L3 attempts to offset reduced choice by wrapping a common domain in a motivating story.

### Risks

- Some students may find the domain irrelevant or uninteresting.
- Assumed cultural or practical familiarity may not be shared.
- A common task can encourage comparison with expected answers rather than exploration.
- The domain may feel like an artificial assessment vehicle.

## Student-Selected Domain Strategy

Lecturer L4 permits domain choice with approval and a default alternative. This supports:

- Personal relevance and ownership
- Authentic stakeholder access
- Variation in requirements and solutions
- Motivation to investigate and revise

### Risks

- Uneven difficulty and project scope
- Different levels of prior domain knowledge
- Greater lecturer preparation and feedback effort
- Harder comparison across students
- Students under assessment pressure may prefer prescribed tasks

The L4 case is feasible partly because the class is small and advanced. It should not be generalised directly to large introductory courses.

## Authenticity as More Than Domain Topic

Authenticity does not result merely from selecting a realistic topic. The data suggest that it also depends on:

- A concrete audience for the model
- Stakeholder interaction or role-play
- Incomplete or changing information
- A reason to justify alternatives
- Iterative feedback and revision
- Integration with later project decisions
- Visible consequences for model quality

A familiar campus application with no model use may be less authentic than a constrained unfamiliar case involving genuine stakeholder reasoning and revision.

## Inclusion and Fairness

Domain choice can unintentionally privilege students with particular cultural, professional, technical, or economic experiences. A supposedly universal example may require hidden background knowledge.

Fair domain design should consider:

- Whether required background knowledge is explicitly supplied
- Whether students have equivalent stakeholder access
- Whether optional choices have comparable scope
- Whether evaluation rewards modelling competence rather than prior domain expertise
- Whether alternative domains are available when a topic is alienating or inaccessible

## Provisional Design Approaches

### Large introductory courses

- Use a common, bounded domain.
- Provide explicit domain orientation and terminology.
- Offer limited choices within a shared structure.
- Use examples with varied surface contexts to test transfer.

### Small or advanced courses

- Permit controlled student choice.
- Require scope approval and comparable modelling deliverables.
- Use stakeholder access and rationale as part of the learning design.
- Provide a default domain for students who prefer structure.

### Mixed approach

- Begin with a shared domain for guided practice.
- Move to bounded choice for later independent work.
- Assess common modelling criteria while allowing contextual variation.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Assignment domains shape representational difficulty because learners must understand a problem before they can abstract it. Familiarity and relevance can reduce entry effort and increase care, while authenticity can make uncertainty and justification educationally meaningful. Course designers must balance these benefits against fairness, complexity control, assessment comparability, and the labour required to support diverse projects.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** domain understanding affects selection, abstraction, and relationship decisions
- **Learner responses and strategies:** exploration, effort, avoidance, and transfer from examples
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** domain orientation, stakeholder elicitation, and bounded examples
- **Social and contextual conditions:** familiarity, interest, access, and prior experience
- **Assessment and course architecture:** common versus selected domains, fairness, scale, and workload
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** authenticity, motivation, care, and perceived usefulness

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Separate domain interest from domain familiarity in participant accounts.
- Examine whether meaningful domains improve model quality or mainly increase effort.
- Identify cases where realistic domains created unnecessary overload.
- Compare the feasibility of choice across cohort sizes and course levels.
- Check whether domain assumptions introduce participation or inclusion barriers.
