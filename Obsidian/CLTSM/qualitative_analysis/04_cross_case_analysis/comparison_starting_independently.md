# Cross-Case Comparison: Starting Independently

## Agreed Interpretation

Starting difficulty is a common early pattern with meaningful variation. It should not be reported as a universal or necessarily dominant difficulty for every student.

## Shared Pattern

Lecturers L1 and L2 describe a gap between understanding notation and independently producing a first model. They observe students waiting for examples, teacher initiation, or confirmation before acting. L3 similarly treats natural-language-to-model synthesis and problem decomposition as central modelling demands.

Several student cases support this pattern:

- S1 describes trial-and-error movement from text to model and requests examples, templates, and incremental instruction.
- S2 learns representational choices largely through examples and analogy.
- S3 needed a worked example before starting during limited prior exposure and continued to seek lecturer confirmation.
- S4 can begin independently but reports lower confidence and prefers private confirmation when a task is ambiguous.

## Variation and Negative Cases

Starting is not the main difficulty for every learner:

- S3 could begin immediately once repeated BPMN exposure had provided familiarity.
- S5 starts readily and instead struggles with refinement, excessive detail, and granularity.
- S6 has a strategy for beginning with stakeholders and goals but struggles later when translating between modelling families.

The category should therefore distinguish:

1. Difficulty initiating a first representation.
2. Difficulty refining or limiting an initial representation.
3. Difficulty translating or maintaining consistency across representations.
4. Difficulty validating whether a defensible representation is good enough.

## Role of Individual Disposition

Individual personality and disposition plausibly contribute to this variation:

- Confidence and willingness to expose incomplete work affect whether students begin publicly or wait.
- Shyness can redirect help-seeking toward trusted peers or private lecturer feedback.
- Comfort with disagreement and critique can support early drafts and iterative correction.
- Fear of being wrong can increase dependence on demonstrations and authority validation.

However, the available data do not support treating personality as a fixed or sufficient explanation. The same behaviour is also shaped by:

- Prior exposure to the notation or modelling family
- Familiarity with the assignment domain
- Availability of peer and lecturer support
- Psychological safety and feedback format
- Whether examples expose reasoning
- Ambiguity and perceived assessment risk
- Group norms about modelling and implementation

Personality should therefore be analysed as interacting with the educational setting rather than as an isolated learner trait.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Starting difficulty commonly appears when learners must commit to an initial representation without a clear correctness signal. Whether this produces waiting, private help-seeking, or immediate experimentation depends on the interaction between learner disposition and situational resources such as prior exposure, feedback safety, peer support, domain familiarity, and assessment risk.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** transforming an uncertain problem into an initial model
- **Learner responses and strategies:** waiting, experimenting, extracting elements, or seeking validation
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** demonstrations, rationale-rich examples, and low-risk feedback
- **Social and contextual conditions:** confidence, shyness, familiarity, peer trust, and prior exposure
- **Assessment and course architecture:** perceived cost of being wrong and opportunities to revise
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** independence, confidence, avoidance, or authority dependence

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Separate participants' own accounts of personality from researcher inference.
- Examine whether confidence changes across a course rather than treating it as stable.
- Compare private, peer, and public starting behaviour.
- Determine whether repeated exposure supports transfer across modelling families or only within one notation.
