# Negative-Case Test: Continuous Assessment and Revision

## Initial Claim

Continuous assessment can improve modelling learning by distributing practice across a course and providing repeated opportunities to demonstrate competence.

## Supporting Evidence

Lecturer L3 reports stronger performance among students following continuous evaluation than among those relying mainly on an exam route.

Potential benefits include:

- Repeated modelling practice
- Earlier detection of difficulties
- Continued engagement throughout the course
- Evidence from several modelling activities
- A balance between group work and individual accountability

## Negative and Limiting Cases

### Evaluation overload

Lecturers L1 and L4 describe students facing many simultaneous:

- Projects
- Tests
- Deadlines
- Grades
- Continuous-assessment tasks across courses

Under these conditions, students may prioritise rapid completion rather than exploration, reflection, or revision.

### Multiple submissions do not guarantee revision

L3 describes several submissions across diagram types, but limited opportunity to revise the same artefact. Students practise repeatedly, yet may not test whether they can apply feedback to improve an existing model.

This distinguishes:

- **Repeated production:** create several models or diagrams.
- **Revision learning:** improve a model after feedback using explicit criteria.

Both can be useful, but they develop different capabilities.

### Scale restricts feedback quality

Large cohorts and broad modelling curricula make detailed feedback expensive. Continuous assessment may increase the number of artefacts without increasing:

- Feedback specificity
- Learner reflection
- Revision depth
- Lecturer availability

### Assessment can create compliance artefacts

Frequent milestones may keep models visible, but they can also produce repeated rubric compliance if models are not used in project decisions.

The relevant distinction is whether a milestone makes the model part of ongoing work or merely creates another deadline.

## Refined Interpretation

> Continuous assessment is beneficial when it creates manageable feedback-revision cycles; frequency alone can produce compliance and overload without deeper modelling competence.

## Feedback-Revision Cycle

A productive cycle includes:

1. Attempt a meaningful modelling decision.
2. Receive timely and criterion-based feedback.
3. Explain or reconsider the original rationale.
4. Revise the same model or decision.
5. Reapply the learning in a later task.

Without these stages, continuous assessment may measure repeated output more than developing judgement.

## Conditions for Productive Continuous Assessment

- A manageable number of assessment points
- Sufficient time between submission and revision
- Feedback focused on transferable criteria
- Opportunities to revise the same representation
- Alignment with real project decisions
- Coordination with wider programme workload
- Feasible lecturer, teaching-assistant, peer, or tool-supported feedback capacity
- Clear distinction between formative and summative activities

## Possible Course Designs

### Fewer, deeper cycles

- Select a smaller number of important modelling artefacts.
- Provide substantive feedback.
- Require justified revision.

### Mixed repetition and revision

- Use short exercises for breadth.
- Use one evolving project model for depth and revision.

### Formative checkpoints

- Provide low-stakes feedback before formal grading.
- Use peer or automated checks for bounded criteria.

### Process-integrated milestones

- Tie model updates to requirement, design, or implementation decisions.
- Ask students to explain what changed and why.

## Distinguishing Outcomes

Continuous assessment may improve:

- Attendance
- Practice frequency
- Submission completion
- Short-term performance

These outcomes should not automatically be interpreted as:

- Independent quality judgement
- Transfer across modelling families
- Ambiguity tolerance
- Long-term model maintenance

The thesis should specify which outcome is supported by each case.

## Revised Proposition

> Continuous assessment supports modelling competence when it provides feasible cycles of attempt, explanatory feedback, justified revision, and later transfer. When assessment points are too dense or feedback capacity is insufficient, continuous evaluation encourages rapid closure and compliance rather than reflective representational judgement.

## Implications

- Prioritise feedback-revision quality over submission frequency.
- Coordinate workload across courses where possible.
- Separate low-stakes practice from summative grading.
- Revise selected models in depth.
- Use scalable feedback only for criteria it can assess reliably.
- Connect milestones to downstream model use.

## Remaining Tests

- Compare repeated-production courses with same-artefact revision courses.
- Distinguish improved grades from improved independent judgement.
- Examine student perceptions of continuous assessment density.
- Identify feasible feedback cycles for large cohorts.
- Test whether process-integrated milestones reduce disposable documentation.
