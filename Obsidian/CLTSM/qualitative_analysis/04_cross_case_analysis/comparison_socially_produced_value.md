# Cross-Case Comparison: Socially Produced Modelling Value

## Agreed Interpretation

> Modelling value is socially produced: shared ownership, peer trust, and integration into team decisions help determine whether a model becomes coordination infrastructure or disposable documentation.

## Meaning of "Socially Produced"

"Socially produced" does not mean that a model is valuable merely because a group likes it, agrees with it, or says that it is useful. It also does not deny that a model has technical properties such as correctness, consistency, or traceability.

The phrase means that a model's practical value partly emerges through what people repeatedly do with it:

- Refer to it when discussing a problem or feature
- Negotiate and record shared decisions through it
- Use it to coordinate responsibilities and implementation
- Explain the system to teammates, newcomers, lecturers, or stakeholders
- Review and challenge its assumptions
- Update it when requirements or implementation change
- Treat maintaining it as legitimate project work

A technically sound diagram may have little practical value if nobody consults, trusts, or maintains it. Conversely, a lightweight or informal model may be highly valuable when it provides a shared and actively maintained representation for collective work.

## Positive Pattern: Model as Coordination Infrastructure

The strongest positive student case is S5:

- The group uses a shared collaborative board.
- Ownership of the model is distributed.
- The model is reviewed through team roles and regular meetings.
- It supports decisions about what the team will build next.
- It continues to be maintained beyond the immediate graded requirement.

Here, the model is not only a deliverable. It becomes infrastructure for discussion, onboarding, consistency checking, and coordination.

Other cases support parts of this pattern:

- S2 describes modelling as a way to explain a complex system to technical and non-technical collaborators.
- S3 reports greater willingness to share with trusted peers and greater care when models have a real analyst audience.
- S6 describes pair ownership and models that synthesise stakeholder information before feature decisions.
- Lecturer L4 uses peer criticism, role-play, and whiteboard interaction to make modelling collaborative and provisional.

## Negative Pattern: Model as Disposable Documentation

S1 and S4 illustrate how models lose force when group practice remains implementation-first:

- Modelling work is delegated to one person.
- Other members treat it as paperwork or a grading requirement.
- The model owner lacks authority to make it central to team decisions.
- Implementation proceeds without consulting the model.
- Updating stops and the model diverges from the system.

The resulting decay is not explained only by individual motivation or notation difficulty. The group has failed to establish a shared process in which the model performs useful work.

## Social Mechanisms

### Shared ownership

Several members can understand, question, revise, and defend the model. Ownership is not equivalent to everyone drawing every element; it means the representation is not isolated with one unofficial specialist.

### Legitimacy

The team recognises modelling as a valid part of engineering work rather than an obstacle delaying implementation.

### Trust and psychological safety

Members can expose incomplete models, disagree about alternatives, and revise decisions without public embarrassment becoming prohibitive.

### Integration into decisions

The model is used during planning, design discussion, implementation coordination, review, or stakeholder communication.

### Maintenance routines

The group has explicit occasions or responsibilities for revising models as understanding changes.

### Audience

A known reader or user gives the model a communicative purpose and influences naming, layout, abstraction, and detail.

## Interaction with Individual Disposition

Personality and confidence affect participation:

- Some students readily present incomplete drafts and learn through public critique.
- Others prefer trusted peers, private feedback, or low-exposure channels.
- Confident students may assume model ownership, but this can still fail if the group does not recognise their authority.

Socially produced value therefore does not require one public participation style. Teams and courses can support different routes into collaborative modelling.

## Interaction with Assessment and Tools

Social practices are shaped by course and technical arrangements:

- Graded incremental milestones may keep models active during a project.
- A final-report-only diagram encourages late documentation.
- Collaborative editors lower participation and updating barriers.
- Version history, comments, and traceability make collective maintenance more feasible.
- Individual grading can reveal competence but may discourage shared ownership if poorly aligned.
- Peer review can develop judgement but may also create bias or competitive pressure.

## Boundary Conditions

- Shared use does not guarantee semantic quality.
- Technically correct models remain necessary where precision matters.
- Not every task requires long-term collaborative maintenance.
- An individual model can still be valuable for personal reasoning.
- Group consensus can preserve a poor decision; critique and external criteria remain necessary.
- Social legitimacy cannot compensate for unusable tools, unclear purpose, or excessive maintenance cost.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> A model becomes practically valuable in collaborative work when participants use it as a shared object for explanation, negotiation, decision-making, and revision. Shared ownership and maintenance connect the representation to ongoing work; when these practices are absent, models are more likely to become isolated deliverables that lose alignment with implementation.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** groups must negotiate competing interpretations and levels of detail
- **Learner responses and strategies:** sharing drafts, peer review, delegation, avoidance, and collective revision
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** critique formats, role-play, and collaborative modelling activities
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** shared editing, comments, traceability, and version history
- **Social and contextual conditions:** trust, ownership, legitimacy, personality, and group norms
- **Assessment and course architecture:** milestones, group assessment, and whether models are integrated into project work
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** coordination infrastructure, maintained artefact, or disposable documentation

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Distinguish model ownership from drawing responsibility in each group case.
- Compare groups where modelling persisted with groups where it decayed.
- Examine whether assessed milestones produce genuine use or only repeated compliance.
- Identify how individual and group assessment affect shared ownership.
- Determine which collaborative practices transfer to professional settings.
