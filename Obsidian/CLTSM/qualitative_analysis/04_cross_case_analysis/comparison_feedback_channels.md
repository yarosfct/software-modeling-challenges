# Cross-Case Comparison: Public, Peer, and Private Feedback

## Agreed Interpretation

> Effective modelling pedagogy should provide multiple feedback channels with equivalent learning value, allowing movement from private or small-group critique toward broader participation without requiring public performance as the price of receiving feedback.

## Why Feedback Format Matters

Modelling feedback often requires learners to expose:

- Incomplete interpretations
- Uncertain relationship choices
- Alternative abstractions
- Possible semantic errors
- Personal rationale

The social risk of exposing unfinished reasoning varies across students. Feedback accessibility therefore depends not only on whether feedback exists, but also on how learners are required to request or receive it.

## Public Critique

### Potential benefits

- Makes alternative approaches visible to the whole class
- Allows comparison of reasoning
- Surfaces misconceptions efficiently
- Develops explanation and defence skills
- Normalises revision
- Can create productive peer learning

S5 provides a strong positive case: public questioning was embarrassing but useful, and repeated exposure increased comfort with sharing drafts.

Lecturer L4 uses whiteboard work and peer criticism to simulate collaborative professional modelling.

### Risks

- Shy or uncertain students may avoid volunteering.
- Public correction may be interpreted as personal failure.
- Fast or confident speakers may dominate.
- Students may wait until they are certain before sharing.
- Participation may measure confidence more than modelling competence.

## Trusted-Peer and Small-Group Feedback

Small-group feedback can:

- Lower exposure risk
- Encourage earlier sharing
- Support collaborative interpretation
- Allow more students to explain decisions
- Build confidence before whole-class discussion

S3 is comfortable sharing with a trusted peer despite reluctance to present publicly. S5's group uses discussion and peer repair as a normal part of modelling.

Risks include uncorrected shared misconceptions, superficial agreement, or unequal participation.

## Private Lecturer or Teaching-Assistant Feedback

Private feedback can:

- Support students who avoid public uncertainty
- Clarify ambiguous expectations
- Provide targeted calibration
- Reveal concerns not raised in class

S4 prefers private confirmation under ambiguity. S2 reports that lecturer validation is available when actively requested.

Risks include:

- Dependence on authority
- Unequal access for students who are less proactive
- Limited scalability
- Useful explanations remaining invisible to peers

## Multiple Equivalent Channels

A diversified design could include:

- Individual draft submission with brief comments
- Pair or small-group model critique
- Anonymous or pseudonymous examples for class discussion
- Digital questions or comments during labs
- Office-hour or teaching-assistant checkpoints
- Whole-class comparison of volunteered or anonymised alternatives
- Written rationale reviewed alongside diagrams
- Structured peer feedback using explicit criteria

"Equivalent learning value" does not mean every channel is identical. Each should provide access to substantive feedback on reasoning and quality, rather than relegating private participants to weaker support.

## Progressive Participation

Participation can be scaffolded:

1. Private self-check or written rationale.
2. Discussion with a trusted peer.
3. Small-group critique.
4. Anonymous class comparison.
5. Voluntary or rotating public explanation.

The objective is to develop communicative confidence without making immediate public performance a prerequisite for learning.

## Feedback Quality

Regardless of channel, useful feedback should:

- Refer to explicit modelling criteria
- Ask for assumptions and rationale
- Distinguish formal errors from defensible alternatives
- Explain consequences
- Permit revision
- Avoid replacing student judgement with a final answer

Channel diversity cannot compensate for feedback that is vague, purely corrective, or limited to grades.

## Inclusion Considerations

Reluctance to speak publicly may arise from:

- Personality or confidence
- Language proficiency
- Prior negative experiences
- Cultural expectations
- Accessibility needs
- Unfamiliarity with the notation
- Power relationships

The thesis should avoid reducing all participation differences to fixed shyness.

## Boundary Conditions

- Professional modelling may require public explanation and disagreement.
- Avoiding all public participation could leave communication skills underdeveloped.
- Anonymous feedback can reduce accountability.
- Peer feedback requires criteria and monitoring.
- Private lecturer feedback is difficult to scale.
- Channel choice should not allow persistent disengagement from collaborative work.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Feedback becomes educationally accessible when learners can expose uncertain modelling decisions at a tolerable level of social risk. Multiple channels allow students to receive substantive critique while gradually developing confidence to explain and defend models more broadly. A single public format may produce rich learning for some students while making feedback effectively unavailable to others.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** feedback addresses uncertain choices and incomplete models
- **Learner responses and strategies:** sharing, withholding, validation seeking, peer discussion, and revision
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** channel design, criteria, and progressive participation
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** anonymous submission, comments, and collaborative review
- **Social and contextual conditions:** confidence, trust, language, familiarity, and psychological safety
- **Assessment and course architecture:** participation grading and scalable feedback structures
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** confidence, independent judgement, inclusion, or withdrawal

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Distinguish preference for private feedback from actual learning outcomes.
- Examine whether students move between channels as confidence develops.
- Compare anonymous, peer, teaching-assistant, and lecturer feedback.
- Identify how channel choice can remain feasible in large cohorts.
- Determine whether participation grading introduces avoidable public-performance pressure.
