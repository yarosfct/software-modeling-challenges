# Provisional Category Framework

## Status

- Accepted as the working cross-case structure on 2026-06-09.
- Contains seven high-level categories.
- Original open codes and memos remain unchanged.
- Category placement may change during axial comparison.

## Relationship to the core phenomenon

The categories organise evidence around the provisional core phenomenon:

> Learning modelling involves making and justifying representational decisions under uncertainty. Difficulties emerge when learners lack sufficient cognitive, pedagogical, social, or technical support for those decisions.

The categories should not be treated as independent topic lists. The later axial analysis must explain relationships among them.

## 1. Representational Challenges

### Analytic focus

Where and why learners struggle to transform, structure, express, connect, refine, translate, or evaluate representations.

### Includes

- Starting from natural-language or incomplete problem descriptions
- Selecting relevant elements and relationships
- Abstraction, decomposition, scope, and granularity
- Notation semantics and diagram-specific reasoning
- Translating between modelling families
- Evaluating multiple defensible alternatives
- Quality, consistency, and traceability judgements

### Excludes

- The learner's response to the challenge, which belongs in Category 2
- The support offered by instructors or tools, which belongs in Categories 3 and 4

## 2. Learner Responses and Strategies

### Analytic focus

How learners act when facing representational uncertainty and what forms of regulation, coping, or avoidance emerge.

### Includes

- Waiting for teacher initiation
- Seeking examples, peers, or private validation
- Trial and error, imitation, and analogy
- Noun-verb or actor-event extraction
- Narrating a model back into plain language
- Simplifying, prototyping, documenting alternatives, and justifying choices
- Self-review, peer review, and traceability checks
- Avoidance, withdrawal, premature coding, or abandoning model maintenance

### Excludes

- Confidence, shyness, group legitimacy, or workload when these operate as conditions; these belong in Category 5

## 3. Pedagogical Scaffolding and Feedback

### Analytic focus

Teaching interventions that make representational reasoning visible, manageable, and progressively independent.

### Includes

- Worked, incomplete, and contrastive examples
- Explanation of rationale rather than presentation of final shapes
- Progressive complexity and decomposition
- Guided-to-independent practice
- Whiteboard demonstrations and critique
- Role-play and stakeholder elicitation
- Feedback timing, revision opportunities, and self-evaluation support
- Private, peer, and whole-class feedback channels

### Excludes

- Formal grading structures and programme-level design, which belong in Category 7
- Tool-provided support, which belongs in Category 4

## 4. Technical Mediation and Tool Support

### Analytic focus

How modelling tools constrain, enable, explain, fragment, or obscure representational work.

### Includes

- Drawing tools versus semantically typed modelling tools
- Syntax and semantic validation
- Executability and simulation
- Error explanation and next-step guidance
- Tool usability, onboarding, cost, access, and reliability
- Collaboration and model maintenance
- Cross-family integration and traceability
- AI as explanation, brainstorming, checking, or substitution risk

### Excludes

- Assessment policy responses to AI, which belong in Category 7
- General group collaboration without a tool mechanism, which belongs in Category 5

## 5. Social and Contextual Conditions

### Analytic focus

Conditions surrounding the learner that shape whether support can be accessed and whether modelling is treated as legitimate activity.

### Includes

- Confidence, insecurity, ambiguity tolerance, and shyness
- Peer trust, group ownership, and implementation-first culture
- Prior modelling and domain exposure
- Domain familiarity and personal relevance
- Attendance, competing workload, and available time
- Class size and access to instructors or teaching assistants
- Professional visibility and expectations around modelling

### Excludes

- Deliberate assignment, curriculum, or assessment choices, which belong in Category 7
- Outcomes such as model decay or perceived usefulness, which belong in Category 6

## 6. Modelling Outcomes and Perceived Value

### Analytic focus

What representational work produces for learning and practice, and how participants consequently value modelling.

### Includes

- Confidence and independence when described as developed outcomes
- Model quality, consistency, readability, and traceability
- Communication, explanation, cognitive offloading, and coordination
- Models as maintained working artefacts
- Models becoming detached from implementation or decaying
- Modelling perceived as meaningful engineering work
- Modelling perceived as arbitrary notation or extra documentation

### Excludes

- Intended learning outcomes stated by instructors unless evidence shows actual consequences

## 7. Assessment and Course Architecture

### Analytic focus

How deliberate curriculum, assignment, and assessment structures shape representational work, learning opportunities, and feasible forms of support.

### Includes

- Curriculum timing and repeated exposure across courses
- Breadth and sequencing of modelling families
- Common versus student-selected project domains
- Authentic, vague, or prescribed assignment briefs
- Individual, group, exam, project, and continuous assessment
- Staged submissions, milestones, feedback cycles, and revision
- Rubrics and whether rationale, alternatives, or self-evaluation are rewarded
- Assessment density and its interaction with exploration time
- Scale, fairness, integrity, and lecturer workload as design constraints
- Assessment changes prompted by generative AI

### Why this is a separate category

The data show recurring mechanisms that cannot be reduced to generic context:

- What is graded influences whether students prioritise notation recall, rationale, quality checking, or model maintenance.
- Staged submissions and revision can turn feedback into learning rather than final judgement.
- Domain policy trades motivation against fairness, comparability, integrity, and lecturer workload.
- Continuous evaluation may improve performance while also creating overload and restricting deep iteration.
- Course scale and curricular breadth determine whether rich feedback and repeated revision are feasible.
- Programme timing affects whether modelling is encountered as an early reasoning practice or a late requirement imposed on code-first learners.

This category is supported across all ten coding sheets, with especially strong evidence in lecturer cases L1-L4 and complementary student evidence about grades, feedback availability, project legitimacy, domains, and incremental milestones.

## Provisional Axial Relationship

The initial relationship among the categories is:

1. **Assessment and course architecture** and **social and contextual conditions** establish the learning environment.
2. Learners encounter **representational challenges** within that environment.
3. They adopt **learner responses and strategies**.
4. **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback** and **technical mediation and tool support** modify what responses are possible and effective.
5. These interactions produce **modelling outcomes and perceived value**.
6. Prior outcomes feed back into confidence, expectations, group legitimacy, and future strategies.

This relationship is provisional and must be tested against each case and negative cases.
