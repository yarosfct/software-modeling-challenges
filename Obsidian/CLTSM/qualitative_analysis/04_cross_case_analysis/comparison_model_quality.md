# Cross-Case Comparison: Developing Model-Quality Judgement

## Agreed Interpretation

Students develop model-quality judgement when formal criteria are translated into actionable checks and repeatedly exercised through feedback, comparison, justification, and revision.

Lecturer validation can contribute to this development, but its role varies between students and situations. It may calibrate emerging judgement, provide reassurance under ambiguity, or become a source of continued authority dependence.

## Lecturer Perspective

Across lecturer cases, model quality includes several recurring dimensions:

- Completeness relative to the problem
- Internal and cross-model consistency
- Semantic correctness
- Readability and simplicity
- Traceability to requirements, goals, code, or tests
- Appropriate abstraction and granularity
- Explanation and justification of modelling decisions

Lecturers do not describe quality as a single correct diagram. Multiple representations may be defensible, but their adequacy must be argued relative to purpose and context.

The lecturer cases also expose a gap between quality being present in assessment and students learning to evaluate their own work:

- L1 grades explicit quality dimensions but reports no explicit model-checking instruction.
- L2 teaches criteria but considers student self-evaluation weak.
- L3 uses executability to make some semantic properties observable, although this introduces additional technical demands.
- L4 emphasises meaning, comparison, rationale, and consistency over notation memorisation.

## Student Perspective

Students convert abstract quality dimensions into practical tests:

- Explain every model element in plain language.
- Recover the original requirements from the model.
- Check whether the diagram communicates its intended message.
- Ask whether a teammate, newcomer, stakeholder, or external reader can understand it.
- Compare names and elements across diagrams.
- Prefer a simpler defensible representation when alternatives exist.
- Document alternatives and justify the selected option.
- Use implementation or a small prototype to expose problematic assumptions.

These practices show that at least some students possess or develop self-evaluation strategies. Their criteria are often more situated and audience-oriented than formal rubrics.

## Role of Lecturer Validation

Lecturer validation can serve several functions:

### Calibration

Feedback helps students understand how abstract criteria apply to concrete modelling decisions. Contrastive examples and rationale-rich comments can turn lecturer judgement into reusable student judgement.

### Reassurance

Students may have a defensible model but still seek confirmation because modelling permits multiple valid alternatives and lacks an immediate compile-like correctness signal.

### Assessment-risk management

Even students who can self-evaluate may seek approval when grades depend on matching lecturer expectations.

### Authority dependence

Validation becomes limiting when students cannot proceed, revise, or accept a justified alternative without teacher confirmation.

The analytic distinction is therefore not simply whether a student asks for lecturer feedback. The important question is whether feedback develops transferable judgement or merely resolves the current task.

## Sources of Variation

The degree of validation dependence appears to vary with:

- Confidence and tolerance for ambiguity
- Prior experience with the modelling family
- Availability and quality of peer review
- Explicitness of quality criteria
- Opportunities to revise after feedback
- Public versus private feedback format
- Perceived assessment consequences
- Whether the model serves a real audience or only a rubric

These relationships should be treated as provisional rather than attributed only to fixed personality traits.

## Provisional Explanatory Statement

> Model-quality judgement develops when learners repeatedly apply explicit criteria to concrete alternatives, explain their reasoning, receive informative feedback, and revise their models. Lecturer validation can scaffold this process by calibrating judgement and reducing uncertainty, but it can also preserve dependence when it supplies answers without making evaluation criteria transferable.

## Category Connections

- **Representational challenges:** deciding whether a model is adequate when alternatives are possible
- **Learner responses and strategies:** self-checking, narration, comparison, justification, peer review, and validation seeking
- **Pedagogical scaffolding and feedback:** actionable criteria, contrastive examples, rationale, and revision
- **Technical mediation and tool support:** syntax checks, semantic validation, executability, and traceability support
- **Social and contextual conditions:** confidence, audience, peer trust, and feedback safety
- **Assessment and course architecture:** what quality dimensions are rewarded and whether revision is possible
- **Modelling outcomes and perceived value:** independent judgement, model usefulness, readability, and continued authority dependence

## Remaining Evidence Checks

- Distinguish quality criteria explicitly taught from criteria inferred through feedback.
- Identify which students use self-checks during modelling versus only before submission.
- Compare quality judgement in individual and group settings.
- Examine whether lecturer validation decreases with experience.
- Determine which feedback forms produce later self-evaluation rather than one-time correction.
