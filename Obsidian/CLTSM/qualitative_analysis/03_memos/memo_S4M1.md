MEMO ID: S4M1
TITLE: Modelling loses force when the group values code over design
DATE: 09/06/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Student interview 4 (reconstructed)
RELATED RQ(s): RQ1, RQ2, RQ3
RELATED CODES: implementation-first group orientation; model as coordination anchor; social-motivational barrier to modelling; model decay after group disengagement; model ownership without group authority
CATEGORY / THEME: Group culture and model legitimacy

1. What is happening here?
S4 describes a group project where the student understood the UML notation and used a class diagram to keep the group aligned, but peers treated the model as unnecessary paperwork and wanted to move directly into Spring implementation.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This case shows that modelling difficulty is not only cognitive or syntactic. A student can know how to model and still stop modelling when the social context does not legitimise the model.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
The underlying issue is a mismatch between model ownership and group authority. S4 effectively owned the model but not the design process, while the rest of the team owned the code and therefore had more influence over what counted as useful work.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "two people in the group wanted to jump directly to implementation in Spring"
- "the diagram was the only thing keeping the group aligned"
- "the hard part was not really the UML notation"
- "the problem was social and motivational"
- "the diagram was very shallow and no longer matched the code"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
The student sees the model as useful for coordination, but the team sees it as paperwork. The model is simultaneously necessary for alignment and socially weak compared with implementation.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
This contrasts sharply with S5, where shared ownership and Miro-based modelling made the diagram a living coordination artefact. It also supports lecturer claims that students often undervalue modelling because they identify useful work with coding.

IMPLICATIONS:
Teaching should not only teach notation but also create project structures where models are collectively owned, reviewed, and used in decisions. Otherwise models may become isolated deliverables maintained by one motivated student.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
How often do students become unofficial "model owners" without real group authority? Do assessed modelling milestones reduce this decay?
