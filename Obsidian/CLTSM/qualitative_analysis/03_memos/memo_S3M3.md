MEMO ID: S3M3
TITLE: Quality is checked by recovering requirements from symbols
DATE: 08/06/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Student interview 3
RELATED RQ(s): RQ1, RQ2
RELATED CODES: symbols-to-words quality checklist; recover requirements from symbols; post-build self-review for correctness; first-option heuristic under uncertainty; changing correct model after self-doubt
CATEGORY / THEME: Modelling process / self-evaluation

1. What is happening here?
The student developed a personal quality strategy: translate model symbols back into words and check whether the original scenario can be recovered. Even with this checklist, the student still waits for professor feedback and has learned to manage uncertainty by avoiding late changes to already-correct models.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This gives a concrete student-side quality criterion that is semantic rather than aesthetic: a model is good enough when it supports requirement recovery. That is close to professional traceability thinking, even if the student does not name it that way.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
Students lack stable internal criteria for correctness when multiple representations seem valid. Self-review helps, but external validation remains necessary, and self-doubt can be harmful if it leads to revising correct work.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "if I transform the symbols into words, can I read the process described in the question through what I modelled?"
- "can I recover all the requirements from the symbols?"
- "I definitely waited for feedback from the professor"
- "I had done it correctly, then I changed some things because I was unsure, and the changed version was wrong"
- "After building the model, I try to read it to check whether it is correct and fix things"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
The student has a thoughtful self-check but still depends on teacher feedback. Personal heuristics reduce anxiety but do not fully replace validation, especially under assessment pressure.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
Compared with student interview 1, where readability anchored quality, S3 adds a stronger bidirectional text-model test. Compared with lecturer interview 2, where student self-evaluation was seen as weak, S3 shows a more explicit personal strategy while still confirming the need for external feedback.

IMPLICATIONS:
Teaching could make requirement-recovery checks explicit as a classroom technique. It may also help to legitimise stable solutions when multiple options appear valid, reducing harmful second-guessing.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Do other students use similar reverse-translation checks? Does this strategy appear more in process-modelling than in UML courses?
