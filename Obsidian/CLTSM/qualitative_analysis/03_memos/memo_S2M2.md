MEMO ID: S2M2
TITLE: Formal frameworks add entry cost to otherwise intuitive modelling
DATE: 23/03/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Student interview 2
RELATED RQ(s): RQ1, RQ3
RELATED CODES: unfamiliar frameworks create friction; framework learning precedes modelling; freeform modelling reduces frustration; frameworks as main deterrent; specific frameworks not always useful; self-made representations feel more intuitive; framework multiplicity overload
CATEGORY / THEME: Learning challenges / notation

1. What is happening here?
The student repeatedly says that formal frameworks made course modelling frustrating. Outside class, where he draws more freely, representation feels more intuitive. This does not mean frameworks are his only difficulty: elsewhere he identifies relationship and representational choices as inherently difficult.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This suggests a distinction between modelling as a useful representational activity and modelling as a framework-bound academic exercise. Part of the resistance is directed toward notation regimes rather than representation itself.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
Formal frameworks introduce an entry cost before representation can begin. Their multiplicity adds memory and transfer demands, while free-form diagrams allow the student to focus directly on the intended message.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "I had to use certain frameworks, which I was not familiar with"
- "I had to learn them before I actually started drawing"
- "I didn't feel that as much... because I didn't use any particular framework"
- "what really put me off about modeling was the frameworks"
- "using a specific framework is not always the most useful"
- "having a lot of different frameworks... I think it's too hard"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
The student acknowledges that standardisation is useful, but experiences framework specificity and multiplicity as barriers. However, his preference for free-form modelling may trade semantic precision for immediate intuitiveness.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
This aligns partly with student interview 1, where notation overload also mattered, but here the critique is sharper and more explicitly directed at frameworks as such.

IMPLICATIONS:
Teaching may need to justify why a framework is worth learning and connect its semantics to a concrete communicative purpose. Otherwise, free-form diagrams may appear more practical despite offering less semantic discipline.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Would students accept formal notations more readily if they encountered fewer frameworks overall? Which parts of framework learning feel most unnecessary to them?
