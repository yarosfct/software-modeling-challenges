MEMO ID: L4M3
TITLE: Vague briefs and stakeholder discovery replace ready-made requirements
DATE: 08/06/2026
INTERVIEW / SOURCE: Lecturer interview 4
RELATED RQ(s): RQ1, RQ2, RQ3
RELATED CODES: intentionally vague problem briefs; requirements discovery and revision; requirements belong to stakeholders not students; stakeholder interviewing required; group-specific specifications from interviews; iterative stakeholder prioritisation
CATEGORY / THEME: Course design / authenticity

1. What is happening here?
The lecturer deliberately gives short, ambiguous problem descriptions so students must discover requirements through stakeholder interviews rather than invent or receive fully specified tasks. Different groups produce different specifications depending on whom they interview and how requirements are prioritised over iterations.

2. Why is this analytically important?
This is a strong authenticity strategy: modelling and requirements work are taught as discovery under uncertainty, not as diagram exercises with known answers. It also makes requirements ownership and stakeholder perspective central.

3. What seems to be the underlying issue?
Students accustomed to well-defined academic problems need to learn that real requirements are partial, revisable, and owned by others. Modelling becomes a way to structure what is learned through social investigation.

4. What evidence supports this?
- "My problem descriptions are intentionally short and vague"
- "requirements must be discovered"
- "the requirements are not their requirements"
- "they need to interview those stakeholders"
- "the specification is not necessarily the same for every group"
- "a second iteration of interviews so the stakeholders can indicate which requirements are most relevant"

5. What tensions / contradictions appear?
Under semester workload pressure, students say they would prefer a better-defined problem and explicit instructions. Discovery-based design conflicts with task-completion coping strategies.

6. How does this relate to other interviews or literature?
Compared with lecturer interview 2, who also emphasised domain understanding as hidden difficulty, L4 makes ambiguity and stakeholder access the main pedagogical device. Compared with student interview 3, who wanted complete examples and clearer theory-practice links, L4's approach is almost the opposite at project level: deliberate openness rather than complete worked cases.

IMPLICATIONS:
Authentic requirements teaching needs scaffolding for interviewing, prioritisation, and living with ambiguity, not just modelling syntax. Students may need support to tolerate under-specification.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
How do students evaluate fairness when specifications diverge across groups? Which scaffolding best helps shy students conduct stakeholder interviews?
