\chapter{Related Work}
\label{cha:related_work}

\section{Scope of the literature}
\label{sec:rw_landscape}

Research on modelling education covers data models, process models, goal models, and model-driven development. Maslov et al.\ map that literature and find it spread across communities that do not share one terminology or one way of evaluating teaching \cite{maslov2025conceptual}. Findings from one family are therefore hard to compare with findings from another. The sections below follow the kinds of work in that map: frameworks for what a course might aim at, studies of where learners struggle, studies of tools, and studies of the course conditions around the task.

\section{Learning objectives}
\label{sec:rw_frameworks}

Several frameworks use Bloom's revised taxonomy to say what a modelling course might aim at beyond copying a notation \cite{krathwohl2002revision}. CaMeLOT combines Bloom's levels with a knowledge dimension and with areas of data-modelling content, and it gives example learning outcomes for designing a course \cite{bogdanova2019camelot}. Bogdanova and Snoeck examined assessment tasks and found that many of them ask students to understand, analyse, and create, while evaluation and metacognition receive little explicit attention \cite{bogdanova2017domain}. Bork relates the same Bloom dimensions to the parts of a modelling method, including the language, the notation, and the procedure, and a teaching case in that paper likewise gives little explicit practice in evaluation \cite{bork2019framework}. These frameworks name a gap in written outcomes. They do not show what the lecturers in this study taught, and this thesis did not sort the interviews into Bloom levels.

Sedelmaier and Landes propose the EVELIN agenda for software engineering competence more broadly \cite{sedelmaier2013research}. Curricula, on that agenda, should state competence targets rather than only lists of topics, and should check those targets over time. The agenda is not about modelling courses. It does treat structural conditions, such as how a programme is organised, as something a competence-oriented curriculum has to take into account.

\section{Reported challenges}
\label{sec:rw_empirical}

Li et al.\ studied beginners learning iStar. They framed tasks with Bloom's levels, analysed the accounts with grounded theory, and reported difficulty understanding constructs, applying the notation, and judging whether a model was complete \cite{istar-learning}. From that they derived support requirements, including explanation of constructs, syntax checking, and guidance through the process, and they implemented some of those requirements in a prototype. The study included lecturers as well as beginners. It ran a tutorial and a modelling session before the interviews, and it stayed with one language. This thesis did not run that session, and it asks about several notations.

Chakraborty and Liebel interviewed students and lecturers about what modelling is for \cite{chakraborty2022perceptions}. Students often did not understand what a model says, and they described modelling either as a useful instrument or as extra documentation.

Chakraborty, Troya, Burgue{\~n}o, and Liebel report a separate study, in which they watched students carry out a modelling task \cite{chakraborty2026actions}. Sixteen pairs at two universities worked for about an hour on a UML class diagram and a sequence diagram. One student drew while the other followed the problem, and then they swapped. The researchers recorded the screen, the pair's talk during the task, and the diagrams they produced, and they analysed the difficulties that showed up in that record. This thesis did not watch its participants model. The interviews are what the participants could still recount about a past incident.

Maslov sets out an agenda for BPMN education rather than the results of a completed experiment \cite{bpmn-education}. The agenda calls for a review of existing work, studies of how novices model and where they go wrong, and BPMN teaching strategies that are then tested. It places process-modelling education among the strands that still need that kind of evidence.

\section{Notation and tools}
\label{sec:rw_tools_mde}

Moody proposes principles for the design of visual notations \cite{moody2009physics}. Symbol overload means that one symbol stands for more than one construct. Symbol redundancy means that more than one symbol stands for the same construct. A diagram that simply contains many symbols is a different problem. These terms describe notations. They are not a result of the interviews in this thesis.

The UML specification separates the abstract syntax of a language from its concrete syntax \cite{omg2017uml}. The abstract syntax is the set of concepts the language allows, and the rules for combining them. The concrete syntax is how those concepts are drawn. Difficulty telling symbols apart is not the same difficulty as failing to understand the concept a symbol stands for. A diagram that follows the rules can still fail to say what the person who made it meant.

Liebel, Badreddin, and Heldal studied how students perceive tools and UML in model-driven courses \cite{liebel2017model}. What students thought of a tool depended on the course around it: the support they were given, the place of the tool in the work, and whether the model was used to talk about requirements or had to generate code. Views of UML were more positive when models were used for requirements and analysis than when they were used mainly to generate code.

Akayama and colleagues discuss how a modelling tool is chosen for a course \cite{akayama2013tools}. The choice depends on the aim of the activity, on what students already know, and on how the course is organised. The paper is a record of that discussion. It does not compare tools by how much students learn.

Hammouda and colleagues asked 44 students about assignments done on paper and assignments done in a modelling tool \cite{hammouda2014tools}. Students reported benefits and limits for both. Paper was used before the tool, and the assignments differed, so the medium alone does not explain the difference. The answers are perceptions, not a measure of learning.

Kienzle and colleagues, and Zschaler and colleagues, list requirements for modelling tools used in teaching, including feedback, examples, and support suited to a course \cite{kienzle2024requirements,zschaler2025tools}. Both papers use one survey of 59 higher-education teachers. They are not two independent studies. A stated priority is not evidence that the feature improves learning. In that survey, support for generative AI was a relatively low priority.

Burgue{\~n}o, Vallecillo, and Gogolla describe teaching UML with OCL so that a tool can execute the model and check constraints \cite{burgueno2018teaching}. A failed check shows that a stated rule was broken. It does not show that the rule, or the model, fits the situation the student was trying to describe. They report using this approach in a course. Students who took the course said they valued it. That report is not a measured gain in learning.

A later agenda from that group sets human factors in model-driven engineering as future research: usability, the effort a tool demands, and whether the tool fits what the user is trying to do \cite{human-factors-mde}. It is a list of questions for the field. It is not a second study of student perceptions.

Buchmann and colleagues argue that a model is not a drawing \cite{buchmann2019conceptual}. They call it a knowledge structure: the symbols have to follow a modelling language, so each mark stands for a concept that language allows, and the links have to be ones that language permits. A tool that only lets someone draw does not, on that account, check whether the model is a valid use of the language.

Hasker and Rowe built UMLint because a student can work for days and then wait again for a grade, and a diagram can follow the notation and still be wrong \cite{hasker2011umlint}. Foss, Urazova, and Lawrence treat slow marking of design diagrams as a bottleneck and offer immediate automated feedback on UML database-design questions \cite{foss2022autoer}. These tools check a diagram against rules. They do not show whether a student can still change the work after a comment.

Vemuri and colleagues report a BPMN course in which students could submit optional practice exercises and receive feedback \cite{vemuri2023feedback}. On average, those exercises drew 11 submissions from 43 students. The report does not show that the feedback improved learning. It does show that feedback can be offered and still go largely unused.

Garaccione and colleagues built a web application in which students draw BPMN diagrams, and they compared two versions of it with 200 students \cite{garaccione2024gamification}. One version added points, progress, and penalties, and it also gave more feedback on the diagram. The other version was the same application with less feedback and without those game-like elements. Students who used the version with points and extra feedback checked the diagram more often, and on one measure the diagrams matched the intended process more closely. The results were mixed, and the authors say the tool still needs further work before ordinary classroom use. The version with the better diagrams had both the extra feedback and the points. The experiment did not include a version with only one of those, so it does not show whether the diagrams were better because of the points, because of the extra feedback, or because of the two together.

Sikkel and Daneva say textbooks rarely teach students to check that two UML diagrams of the same system agree, and they use exercises that compare a pair of diagrams \cite{sikkel2010consistency}. Verbruggen and Snoeck watched students model one case as a class diagram and as a BPMN model \cite{verbruggen2022multiperspective}. Deciding which requirements belong in which diagram is, on their account, part of the quality of the model.

\section{Domain, motivation, and authenticity}
\label{sec:rw_human_context}

Gra{\ss}l and colleagues surveyed students and educators about the domain of a modelling assignment, about motivation, and about inclusion \cite{grassl2026dei}. Students preferred domains they found personally meaningful. Educators' assumptions about which domains would motivate students, such as games or technical systems, did not always match those preferences. The survey is a reason to pay attention to the domain participants describe. It does not show that a particular domain causes students to be excluded, or that a familiar domain improves learning.

Holmes et al.\ studied software engineering education more broadly \cite{holmes2018dimensions}. They describe the experiences students value as real projects, real tasks, and real mentors. The work is not about modelling notations. It is about the setting of the task.

Whittle and colleagues report on a software engineering course they taught at Lancaster University \cite{whittle2014studio}. They call the course a design studio: students work on a practical project, with coaching and critique, instead of lectures being the main activity. The name is borrowed from studio teaching in design schools. When students could choose how to draw, they made many informal drawings, sketches that do not follow a notation such as UML, and they almost never produced a formal model. They used those sketches when talking with each other about the design. An informal drawing can still be useful in that conversation even when it is not maintained as a formal model. The paper concerns this one course. It does not examine which assignment domain students prefer, and it does not examine inclusion.

\section{Groups and generative AI}
\label{sec:rw_groups_ai}

Silva and colleagues compared individual and group UML exercises \cite{silva2018group}. On some measures the individual diagrams were more correct. On some measures the group diagrams were more complete. A group is not automatically the better way to model.

C\'amara and colleagues tested ChatGPT on UML class diagrams with OCL and found the results unreliable next to code generation: problems of syntax, inconsistency, and scale \cite{camara2023chatgpt}. That is a test of the system on those tasks. It is not a study of how students in a course used it.

\section{How studies of this kind are done}
\label{sec:rw_competence_methods}

Stol, Ralph, and Fitzgerald review how grounded theory has been used in software engineering and set out guidelines for doing it \cite{stol2016grounded}. Hoda proposes socio-technical grounded theory for that field \cite{hoda2018socio}. The explanation has to cover the people, the way they work, and the tool they use. The tool is part of what is explained, not a mere setting around it. Adolph, Hall, and Kruchten give a practical account of using grounded theory to study how software is developed \cite{adolph2011using}.

This thesis used the parts named in Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}: open coding, memos written during coding, and constant comparison. It did not use the Strauss and Corbin coding paradigm.

\section{What this thesis adds}
\label{sec:rw_summary_positioning}

Studies cited above already include interviews with students and lecturers, comparisons of classroom tasks, and tests of tools. This thesis relates the difficulties its participants could still describe to the course conditions they described. A missing path and difficulty with the notation are reported as different difficulties and are not ranked. Comments sometimes arrived while the work could still change. In the group episodes, what happened to the diagram went together with how the work was shared and what the model was for. It does not measure how common those difficulties are, and it does not test whether a change in the course or the tool would help.
