\section{Software modelling}
\label{sec:software_modelling_conceptual}

\subsection{Purpose and role of modelling in software engineering}
\label{subsec:purpose_role_modelling_se}


A software model shows the parts of a system, or of the problem, that matter for a purpose, such as talking about the system or trying a design \cite{kuhne:DagSemProc.04101.15,gorschek2014design,petre2013uml}. The symbols have an agreed meaning: the same mark is read in the same way. The language is the set of allowed symbols and those meanings. A model is one description written in that language for one problem \cite{kuhne:DagSemProc.04101.15}.

\subsection{Conceptual modelling as a broader discipline}
\label{subsec:conceptual_modelling_broader_discipline}

Conceptual modelling is the wider family, including data, process, and goal models. This thesis is about software modelling. The courses use some of those notations, such as UML, BPMN, entity-relationship models, and goal models.

\subsection{Modelling methods, languages, and artefacts}
\label{subsec:modelling_methods_languages_artefacts}

One account of a modelling method includes the language and the steps for creating a valid model from a description. It also includes what a tool can do with that model once it exists, such as simulating it or turning it into another form \cite{bork2019framework}. The language is the allowed symbols and what they mean. What they mean is called the semantics. Students can copy the symbols and still be unable to take those steps or judge the result.

If a model is stored so a machine can read it, it can be queried, analysed, or transformed.

\subsection{Modelling education}
\label{sec:modelling_education}

Modelling education, in this thesis, means the courses in which students learn software modelling: what a construct means, how to build a model from a description, and how to judge whether it is good enough. Students do not all treat those courses as equal to their programming courses. Chakraborty and Liebel found that students describe modelling either as a useful instrument or as extra documentation \cite{chakraborty2022perceptions}.

\subsection{Modelling tools}
\label{subsec:tools_mde_education}

A modelling tool is the program a student uses to draw a model, to check it, or to turn it into another form, such as code. In model-driven engineering, the tool treats the model as the source of that other form.

\subsection{Implications for the scope of this thesis}
\label{subsec:implications_scope_thesis}

This thesis is about software modelling in courses, where students and lecturers deal with what a construct means, how to build a model from a description, and how to judge whether it is good enough.

\section{Bloom's revised taxonomy}
\label{sec:blooms_taxonomy}

Bloom's revised taxonomy names six levels: remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, and create \cite{krathwohl2002revision}. This thesis uses four of them for interview prompts: remembering a notation, understanding a construct, applying a procedure, and evaluating whether a model is good enough. Metacognition means noticing one's own way of working.

\section{Grounded Theory and Socio-Technical Grounded Theory}
\label{sec:grounded_theory}

Grounded Theory is a way of developing an explanation from qualitative material, of which interviews are one example, rather than from a hypothesis stated in advance and then tested \cite{glaser1967discovery,stol2016grounded}. In this thesis the material is the interviews. The explanation is built from what the participants say, and from comparing those accounts with one another. The categories are not fixed in advance and then used as boxes to sort the interviews into.

Grounded Theory was first set out by Glaser and Strauss, in sociology, as a way to develop an explanation from the material rather than to test a hypothesis already in hand \cite{glaser1967discovery}. Later variants kept that aim and changed the procedure. Strauss and Corbin relate categories through a fixed set of headings, such as conditions, actions, and consequences \cite{strauss1998basics}. Charmaz treats the explanation as an interpretation the researcher builds with the participants, not as something simply found in the data \cite{charmaz2006constructing}. This thesis uses Socio-Technical Grounded Theory, described below, because the interviews are about people working with modelling tools, and that method was written for software engineering so that the tool stays inside the explanation.

Three steps carry the comparison. In open coding, the researcher writes a separate note for one matter in what was said, for example the notation, the tool, or the feedback, and points that note back to the place in the interview. A memo links the notes that belong to one idea. Constant comparison means that each new note is set against the notes already written, so that similar matters can be brought together and matters that only look similar can be kept apart. Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology} describes how those steps were carried out in this study.

Socio-Technical Grounded Theory (STGT) was proposed for software engineering research \cite{hoda2018socio}. It uses the same steps, and it was written for this field because the explanation has to cover the people, the way they work, and the tool they use. The tool is part of what is explained, not a mere setting around it. A modelling task is a case of this: the difficulty can sit in the notation, in what the lecturer did, or in the tool, and those are not separated into a human story with the tool left outside it.

This study did not use the Strauss and Corbin coding paradigm. Here, categories were related by comparing incidents and by the findings map, as Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology} describes. The four further interviews were with students who were available and had done software modelling. They were not picked so that each person would answer one particular open question. The study also does not claim that interviewing had exhausted every detail of the categories.

Interviewing stopped after the four further interviews. They did not add a 15th category. At that point the 14 categories were judged enough for the research questions of this thesis. Further details could still appear. Further interviews were also difficult to arrange in the time available, as Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology} states. The study does not claim that the categories were saturated. That stop is not, by itself, evidence that every relation inside the categories is settled.

\section{Human factors}
\label{sec:human_factors}

Human factors are the conditions around a task that change how a person does it. In this thesis they are the conditions around a modelling task in a course: the tool, the subject of the assignment, who reads the model, the feedback, and the load of the course. They are not the notation itself. A heavy tool, an uninteresting domain, the absence of a reader, a comment that arrives after the work can no longer change, or a heavy course load can each change what the student is able to do with the model. Chapter~\ref{cha:related_work} reviews studies of those conditions. Chapters~\ref{cha:findings} and~\ref{cha:discussion} describe what the participants said about them.
