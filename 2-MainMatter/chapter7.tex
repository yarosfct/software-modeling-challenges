\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{cha:conclusion}

This thesis asked how software modelling is experienced, taught, and learned in higher education, treating modelling as a socio-technical activity rather than as notation drill alone. Fourteen interviews (four lecturers, ten students) were analysed with a lightweight STGT procedure: open coding, short memos, constant comparison, and a findings map. The result is an empirically grounded account of \textbf{14 categories} and \textbf{four phenomena}, sufficient for the research questions of this MSc. The four phenomena in Chapter~\ref{cha:findings} are claims that several categories support together (Figure~\ref{fig:findings_map}). PH1 is the first diagram. PH2, PH3, and PH4 bring together strategies, support needs, and course conditions.

\section{Answers to the research questions}
\label{sec:conc_rqs}

On RQ1, the main challenges are a gated first modelling move, difficulty with unfamiliar or lookalike notation as a distinct problem, difficulty judging ``good enough'' when the modelling tools the participants described often did not compile or simulate the model, and breakdowns when students switch notation families. A compile check would show that a model is well-formed, and would leave open whether the diagram says what the person who made it meant.

On RQ2, worked examples and guided walkthroughs remain a main way the work starts. L4 guides students as they solve the exercise, rather than solving it for them first. Students also described their own procedures for getting a first diagram started. The interviews do not show that these courses taught no process. A comment could still change the open work, and who could get it depended on the channel. In one case the grade came before the comments, so that delivery could not be resubmitted. Where students used AI, they asked it to explain or compare, and S5 also asked for alternative class diagrams and then rewrote the graded model rather than handing in the generated output.

On RQ3, checks and guidance are wanted. Wanting a check did not decide what happened to the tool. A group can stop refining a diagram, rebuild it, or add a review so the members can explain it. S4 reported that an interesting domain matters. S7 and S10 described a course submission beside a model they had to explain at a defence. Those reports are not ranked. Heavy workloads and continuous assessment leave less time for worked examples and for feedback while the model can still change. L1 said classes are not long enough to cover every example, and that students sometimes skip a class to study for a test.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:conc_contributions}

The contribution is a bounded, traceable map of modelling-education challenges and support needs that connects student and lecturer voices to the socio-technical conditions of courses, tools, groups, and feedback. Relative to method-specific error taxonomies and Bloom-based learning-outcome frameworks, the account splits mechanisms that are often collapsed (a missing path versus difficulty with the notation, which are not ranked; when a comment can still be used versus who can get it; checks and guidance, which did not decide what happened to the tool) and treats a change of notation family as its own difficulty, including that leaving a concept out can be the right move. Chapter~\ref{cha:discussion} reads these patterns as possible changes to courses and tools. This study did not test whether those changes would help. The interviews do not show that lecturers fail to teach, comment, or relate one language to another. A comment can still leave the model unchanged. A student can still miss how the next language relates to one they already know.

\section{Limits}
\label{sec:conc_limits}

Interviewing stopped after the four further interviews. They did not add a 15th overview category. The study does not claim that the categories were saturated. That stop is not, by itself, evidence that every relation inside the categories is settled. Further details could still appear. The second round was sought because open questions remained. The students who took part had done software modelling and were available. They were not chosen one by one to match each open question. The limit on arranging further interviews is stated in Section~\ref{sec:method_rigour}. No further lecturers were interviewed. Several open questions remain labelled for later work (Table~\ref{tab:open_questions}). What transfers is the set of conditions under which a category appeared. What those procedures do to particular claims is stated in Chapter~\ref{cha:discussion}.

\section{Closing}
\label{sec:conc_closing}

Future work can take up three questions this thesis leaves open. One is whether ``students wait to start'' describes a whole class or only some students in it. That would need the lecturer and the students from the same course, or a look at the first minutes of a lab. Another is whether, in a course that uses more than one language, students were shown how the next language relates to the previous one before they had to move from one language to the other. A third is whether students, in their own words, describe difficulty with abstraction as a lack they have. Lecturers in this study described that difficulty. Students did not describe themselves that way. S6 and S10 did talk about what a diagram should leave out, and about how much detail is enough. The detail is in Section~\ref{sec:disc_future}. Those questions are a different project from the account this thesis has already produced.
