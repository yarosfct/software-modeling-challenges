\chapter{Methodology}

\section{Research design and rationale}
\label{sec:method_design}

This thesis investigates how software modelling is experienced, taught, and learned in higher education settings. The focus is on modelling as a socio-technical activity, encompassing conceptual understanding, procedural strategies, tool mediation, and educational and organisational constraints. Since these phenomena are not well explained by existing theory and are strongly context-dependent, the study adopts a qualitative, theory-building research design.

The study employs a Grounded Theory (GT) approach, which is well-suited to under-theorised problem areas where the goal is to generate an explanatory account grounded in empirical data such as participants' experiences and perspectives, rather than testing pre-existing hypotheses \cite{stol2016grounded,adolph2011using}. To explicitly account for the interactions between social and technical elements (e.g., notations, tools, course structures, and stakeholder perspectives), the analysis is informed by Socio-Technical Grounded Theory (STGT) \cite{hoda2018socio}. The study design follows an iterative logic in which data collection and analysis proceed in parallel, allowing emerging insights to guide subsequent sampling and questioning \cite{adolph2011using,stol2016grounded}.

In addition, the methodology draws inspiration from prior empirical studies on modelling education, particularly the iStar learning study, which combined task-based learning activities with semi-structured interviews and grounded theory analysis to derive a structured set of challenges and corresponding support requirements \cite{istar-learning}. The present work adapts and extends this approach to a broader scope beyond a single modelling method, while maintaining the strengths of an activity-centered elicitation design and a systematic theory-building analysis.


\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:method_rq}

The study is guided by the following overarching research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What challenges do students and instructors perceive in learning and teaching software modelling in higher education, and how do these challenges manifest across different modelling activities (e.g., interpreting, creating, refining, and evaluating models)?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} What strategies do students and instructors employ to address these challenges, and what support needs (pedagogical and tool-related) do they identify?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} How do socio-technical factors (e.g., tools, course design, domains, feedback practices) shape modelling learning and teaching experiences?
\end{itemize} 

These questions are exploratory and explanatory in nature, aiming to uncover the lived experiences of key stakeholders (students and instructors) and making them suitable for a grounded-theory-based methodology \cite{stol2016grounded,hoda2018socio}.

\section{Study context and participants}
\label{sec:method_context_participants}

The study is conducted in the context of higher education software engineering and related courses that include software modelling components, such as requirements engineering, software design, business process modelling, or model-driven engineering. Two participant groups are targeted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Students:} learners who have encountered modelling activities in at least one course and have completed at least one modelling-related assignment or project.
    \item \textbf{Instructors:} lecturers or teaching assistants who have taught modelling-related content and have experience assessing student models or guiding modelling activities.
\end{itemize}

Sampling follows the principle of theoretical sampling: initial participants are recruited to seed the analysis, and subsequent recruitment is guided by emerging categories (e.g., differences between courses, notations, or tool ecosystems) until categories are sufficiently elaborated \cite{adolph2011using,stol2016grounded}. To ensure informative accounts, inclusion criteria emphasise participants with direct experience of modelling tasks rather than only theoretical exposure.

\section{Data collection}
\label{sec:method_data_collection}

Data collection consists of semi-structured interviews complemented by an activity-centered elicitation component. This combination is intended to elicit both reflective accounts and concrete, situated examples of modelling practice, following the logic used in prior modelling education research \cite{istar-learning}. The procedure was refined through a pilot to ensure clarity, timing, and depth of questioning.

\subsection{Instruments and pilot}
\label{subsec:method_instruments_pilot}

Two semi-structured interview guides were developed: one for students and one for instructors. The guides share a common backbone (perceptions of modelling, challenges, strategies, support needs) but differ in emphasis: student interviews focus on learning experiences and task-level difficulties, whereas instructor interviews focus on teaching goals, scaffolding, assessment, and observed misconceptions. The pilot interview phase was used to validate the interview flow, estimate duration (20--30 minutes), and improve probing questions for eliciting concrete examples and contextual detail.

Bloom-based modelling frameworks also informed the formulation of prompts by mapping modelling competence to observable activities (e.g., interpreting models, constructing models from text, and judging model quality), supporting a balanced coverage of cognitive demands without imposing Bloom as a coding template.

\subsection{Activity-centered elicitation: modelling task}
\label{subsec:method_modelling_task}

To move beyond abstract opinions, participants are invited to discuss a concrete modelling task or artefact. Depending on participant context, this includes one of the following:
(i) a short modelling exercise based on a brief scenario, or
(ii) a retrospective walkthrough of a recent modelling assignment or model created/assessed in a course.

This activity-centered elicitation is inspired by the iStar learning study, which used a tutorial and modelling session followed by an interview to anchor participant reflections in a concrete modelling experience \cite{istar-learning}. The goal is to surface tacit reasoning, decision points, typical errors, and tool- or feedback-related constraints that may not emerge from interview questions alone.

\subsection{Interviews}
\label{subsec:method_interviews}

Interviews are conducted individually and last approximately 20--30 minutes. Student interviews cover background, perceived usefulness of modelling, learning difficulties, strategies for going from text to model, evaluation criteria, tool experiences, and motivational and contextual factors (including inclusion-related experiences where relevant). Instructor interviews address teaching objectives, scaffolding practices, assessment design, common student misconceptions, tool choices and constraints, and perceived gaps in current pedagogy. Interviews include flexible probing to explore emerging themes in depth, consistent with GT's iterative and adaptive logic \cite{adolph2011using,stol2016grounded}.

\section{Data analysis}
\label{sec:method_analysis}

\subsection{Grounded Theory variant and socio-technical stance}
\label{subsec:method_gt_variant}

The analysis follows Grounded Theory principles as adapted for software engineering research \cite{stol2016grounded,adolph2011using}. In particular, the study adopts a socio-technical lens aligned with STGT, treating modelling education as an interaction between social elements (students, instructors, feedback practices, course constraints) and technical elements (notations, tools, model artefacts, automation features) \cite{hoda2018socio}. This stance informs both the coding process and the interpretation of emerging categories.

\subsection{Coding process}
\label{subsec:method_coding}

The coding process follows an iterative progression of open coding, axial coding, and selective coding. Open coding is used to label segments of interview transcripts and artefacts with conceptual codes capturing modelling challenges, strategies, perceptions of value, tool interactions, and contextual constraints. Constant comparison is applied throughout: newly coded incidents are compared to previous incidents and codes to refine categories and identify variation \cite{adolph2011using}.

Axial coding is used to relate categories using a coding paradigm (e.g., phenomenon, causal conditions, context, intervening conditions, and strategies), as demonstrated in modelling education research on iStar learning \cite{istar-learning}. Selective coding is then employed to integrate categories into a coherent explanatory account. Following guidance for GT in SE, memoing is used continuously to document analytic decisions, emerging hypotheses, and links between categories \cite{stol2016grounded,adolph2011using}.

\subsection{Theoretical sampling and saturation}
\label{subsec:method_sampling_saturation}

Sampling decisions are guided by emerging categories rather than predetermined demographic quotas. For example, if early analysis suggests that tool frustration is a major driver of negative modelling attitudes, additional participants may be recruited from courses with different tool ecosystems to explore variation. Data collection continues until categories are sufficiently elaborated and additional data no longer contribute new properties or relationships to the core explanatory account \cite{adolph2011using,stol2016grounded}.

\subsection{Bloom's taxonomy as a sensitising concept}
\label{subsec:method_bloom_sensitising}

Bloom's revised taxonomy is used as a sensitising concept to support interpretation and communication of findings, particularly when describing the cognitive nature of modelling challenges (e.g., understanding modelling constructs, applying modelling procedures, analysing model quality) \cite{krathwohl2002revision}. Importantly, Bloom is not imposed as a rigid coding template; instead, grounded categories remain primary, and Bloom-inspired language is used where it aligns with participant accounts and helps structure the presentation of results, consistent with GT guidance to avoid forcing data into preconceived frameworks \cite{stol2016grounded}.

\section{Ethics and data management}
\label{sec:method_ethics}

Participation is voluntary and based on informed consent. Interview recordings and transcripts are anonymised, and any potentially identifying details (e.g., course identifiers, project names, personal data) are removed or generalised. Data are stored securely and used only for the purposes of this research. When collecting modelling artefacts (e.g., student models or assignments), additional care is taken to remove identifying information and to ensure that artefacts are used in ways consistent with participants' consent and institutional policies.

\section{Methodological limitations}
\label{sec:method_limitations}

As a qualitative, context-sensitive study, the findings aim for analytical rather than statistical generalisation. Participant accounts may be influenced by recall bias, social desirability, and variation in course contexts. Additionally, tool- and notation-specific experiences may limit transferability across institutions or modelling families. These limitations are mitigated by triangulating accounts with concrete artefacts where possible, sampling for variation as categories emerge, and explicitly documenting contextual factors that shape the observed phenomena \cite{stol2016grounded,hoda2018socio}.
