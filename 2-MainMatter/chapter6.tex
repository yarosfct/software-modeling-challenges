\chapter{Discussion}
\label{cha:discussion}

This chapter interprets the 14 categories and four phenomena against the research questions and against related work. What Chapter~\ref{cha:findings} reports from the interviews stays what the participants said. Where this chapter explains what those reports might mean, it says that the explanation is a reading. An implication is a proposal, and it was not tested. Open questions stay open.

\section{Answering the research questions}
\label{sec:disc_rqs}

\subsection{RQ1 --- Challenges across modelling activities}

The challenges that earned overview categories are not a single ``students struggle with UML'' story. Starting is gated when a worked path is missing or arrives after a project already required models (CAT1). That gate is distinct from difficulty with the notation itself (CAT2). The UML specification separates concrete syntax, how a concept is drawn, from abstract syntax, the concepts the language allows and the rules for combining them \cite{omg2017uml}. S1 could name the task and still not know what the marks meant. S8's requirements diagrams and class diagrams looked alike while standing for different concepts. Moody calls one symbol that stands for several constructs symbol overload \cite{moody2009physics}. These interviews show unfamiliar marks, similar-looking diagrams, and a lot of information to absorb. They do not show that one symbol was standing for several constructs. In this study, models and tools often did not compile or simulate execution (CAT12). A compile check would show that a model is well-formed, and would leave open whether the diagram says what the person who made it meant. Burgue{\~n}o, Vallecillo, and Gogolla make that distinction when teaching UML with OCL: a failed check shows that a stated rule was broken, not that the model fits the situation \cite{burgueno2018teaching}. Switching notation families is a further challenge: locally sensible diagrams lose trace or answer the wrong question (CAT14).

PH1 is the difficulty of making a first model from a written problem statement. Difficulty with the notation is a separate account. S3 said it was the bigger initial problem. The interviews do not rank the two. The interviews do not show that the lecturers in this study failed to teach that step. Four lecturers cannot support that claim. A lecturer may teach the step, and a student may still not learn it. One reading of the same difficulty is that the student has to understand the problem, decide what this kind of diagram is for, and leave out what that diagram does not need. A class diagram and a goal model do not keep the same details. That reading is an interpretation. It is not what the interviews, by themselves, establish.

This pattern is consistent with published modelling-education work that reports difficulty moving from requirements to models and judging adequacy \cite{istar-learning,maslov2025conceptual,chakraborty2022perceptions}, but it is not a replication of an iStar-only error taxonomy. Li et al.\ studied novices in one goal-modelling language and derived teaching and tool requirements from that setting \cite{istar-learning}. The present corpus spans BPMN, UML, iStar, ER, and courses that teach several of those notations together, and it includes lecturers. The closest empirical overlap is the first-modelling-move problem (PH1) and the demand for support that explains constructs rather than only listing them. Divergence appears where this study splits start-gate from difficulty with the notation, and where CAT14 treats family translation as its own challenge rather than as more notation learning.

CaMeLOT and related Bloom-informed frameworks argue that modelling education under-specifies evaluation and metacognitive outcomes \cite{bogdanova2019camelot,bogdanova2017domain,bork2019framework}. In this study, students judged a model by walking through it, by justifying each element, or with a list of their own, such as whether every element could be explained, whether the diagram matched the written requirements, and whether it was readable (CAT12, PH2). A teacher's comment was the other way they learned whether it was good enough. A checklist from the instructor might still not be enough. If the list only checks that the model is well-formed, a student can take a correct result as a finished model when the diagram does not yet say what they meant. It can be syntactically in order and still be incomplete. That is a limit of a validity list, not a finding that an instructor's list misled someone in this study. Those frameworks are tools for writing course learning outcomes. This study did not sort interviews into Bloom levels (Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}).

One reading of these checks is that the student took up a way of judging that the class had already shown. S7 said the list used in tests, requirements in ordinary language and then crossed off, might have been what the practicals teacher did on the board. S6 was taught completeness, consistency, and justification, and the personal check was to walk through the model and say why each element is there. S10, after getting a boundary wrong, started asking who is external before drawing the next BPMN, and reviewed the model before asking an AI tool. S3 turned the symbols back into words and checked whether the process in the question came back. Another reading is that the student made a list because nobody had said what counted as good enough, or that the list only ticks off the assignment. S4 had a personal list and said nobody had given it as a rubric. A third reading is that a short or missing list is itself a way of teaching. The student has to make a check that fits how they work, and being able to do that is part of what the course is for. S7's practicals teacher kept saying that more than one model can be right. The interviews do not show that a lecturer left the list out for that reason. S6's personal walkthrough sat on top of quality ideas that had been taught, so a check of one's own can grow out of teaching as well as out of a gap. The interviews do not show where each list came from, and they do not show that the list made the model better.

\subsection{RQ2 --- Strategies and support needs}

Worked examples and board walkthroughs were the main way the work got started (CAT3). L4 used another walkthrough: students solved the exercise with the lecturer, who guided them, rather than the lecturer solving it first. Students also described procedures for getting from the problem to a first diagram (CAT4), including reading the brief aloud, starting quickly in case of being called to the board, and working in a notebook before comparing it with the board. Some of those procedures were part of what the class was doing. The interviews do not show that these courses taught no process. S8 said a course may have explained modelling, and that they either missed it or have forgotten. Feedback is both a timing problem (CAT5) and an access problem (CAT6). Where students used AI, they asked it to explain or compare, and S5 also asked for alternative class diagrams and then rewrote the graded model rather than handing in the generated output (CAT13).

Support needs that participants articulated therefore cluster around (i)~seeing a mapping from text to model, (ii)~getting a correctness signal while the artefact can still change, and (iii)~getting that signal in a format they will actually use (desk walk-around versus raising a hand; private versus public). Tables~\ref{tab:cat5_rework} and~\ref{tab:cat6_access} summarise the wave~2 variation behind (ii) and (iii). Research on teaching with modelling tools similarly argues for feedback written for learners, not the cryptic error messages of an industrial compiler \cite{liebel2017model}. Delayed instructor critique of student diagrams is itself a documented bottleneck, which automated checkers try to shorten \cite{hasker2011umlint,foss2022autoer}. Vemuri et al.\ report optional BPMN exercises with feedback that still drew few submissions relative to the class \cite{vemuri2023feedback}. Offering feedback is not the same as students using it. This study adds the access dimension: the same walk-around that helps a reserved student is not the same as a public board that an outgoing student enjoys, and neither is the same as comments that arrive after the grade is locked. Those comments are not only the teacher's. L4 had groups work the same problem and then check one another's models. S10's group later added a review so the members could explain the diagrams. Students also checked their own models. The interviews do not show that every course asked for that review, or that every student did it with care. They do show that a classmate can be the person who looks at the model.

CAT13 should not be over-read as an AI-education finding. Four student cases did not hand in a generated model as the graded work. S5 asked for alternative class diagrams and then rewrote the model. That aligns with PH3 (checks and explanations, not auto-drawn homework) and with assessments of ChatGPT on UML that report limited reliability compared with code generation \cite{camara2023chatgpt}. It does not evaluate any particular tool. L3's wish for an LLM that explains a student's model back, rather than writing it, is a support need L3 stated, which was not tested, and it is not part of the student uses in CAT13.

One reading of S10's use is a trade-off. S10 made the model, reviewed it, and then sometimes sent a screenshot and asked an AI tool to find inconsistencies or strange notation. S10 checked the suggestions again. If the tool and S10 disagreed, S10 went to the slides, the documentation, or the teacher. S10 also said that small questions to the teacher became less frequent, and that a discussion with the teacher sometimes taught more. The tool kept S10 from staying blocked. It also took the place of some of those questions. Another reading is that checking the suggestion still depends on the student already knowing enough to notice a wrong answer. The interviews do not show that the tool's answer was more reliable than a generated diagram.

\subsection{RQ3 --- Socio-technical conditions}

Tools, groups, audiences, and course load co-shape whether modelling happens at all. Checks and guidance are wanted (CAT7). Wanting a check did not decide what happened to the tool (CAT8). A group can stop refining a diagram, rebuild it, or add a review so the members can explain it (CAT9). S4 reported that an interesting domain matters. S7 and S10 described a course submission beside a model they had to explain at a defence. Those reports are not ranked (CAT10). Load and theory--practice gaps leave less time for the demonstrations and the comments that CAT3 and CAT5 describe (CAT11).

Liebel et al.\ already showed that tool perception depends on usability, the course role of models, and support---not brand alone---and that industrial tools can demotivate when models must generate code precisely and help is unreliable \cite{liebel2017model}. CAT7 versus CAT8 is the same tension in interview form: participants want checks. S7 tried an outdated compile interface and then dropped it. L1 reported that licensing can still force a tool change. L4 reported that switching tools during the course creates friction. Buchmann et al.\ argue that models are knowledge structures, not drawings \cite{buchmann2019conceptual}. CAT14's ``models answer different questions'' is a student-language version of that claim at family boundaries, consistent with teaching materials that treat cross-diagram consistency and UML--BPMN perspectives as learnable content rather than as extra symbols \cite{sikkel2010consistency,verbruggen2022multiperspective}. S6 said that relation had not been explained. S10 was told after the diagram had gone wrong. That is what those two accounts report.

Classroom comparisons of group versus individual UML exercises find that groups are not uniformly better on correctness and completeness \cite{silva2018group}. CAT9 does not score correctness. It reports that refinement stopped, a system was rebuilt, or a review was added so the group could explain the diagrams. Those are different outcomes. The group-exercise result does not explain what happened in S4, S7, or S10.

Survey work on domain choice finds that students prefer personally meaningful domains and that educator assumptions about games and technical domains can miss that preference \cite{grassl2026dei}. CAT10 reports both. S4 said an interesting domain makes a difference. S7 and S10 described a course submission, which a teacher already reads, beside a model they had to explain at a defence. The interviews do not separate those influences, and they do not show that one of them raised model quality.

One reading of these accounts is that the use of the model changed. A teacher already reads a course submission. S7 described course models that would probably only pass through the teacher's hands, and a thesis model that had to explain a system to a supervisor and at a defence. S10 treated difficulty explaining the diagram as a reason to simplify it: if the people who made it could not follow it, it was probably too complicated. S4's check included whether the diagram was readable enough for a supervisor, and S4 also said an interesting domain makes a difference. Another reading is that the extra care came from the grade, from the size of the thesis system, or from the domain. The interviews do not separate those. They do not show that any one of them raised the quality of the model.

Maslov's picture of conceptual modelling education as fragmented across notations describes how that research is published \cite{maslov2025conceptual}. It does not explain why a student had trouble moving from one diagram to another. CAT14 is that student-level account.

\section{Implications}
\label{sec:disc_implications}

The following implications are \emph{not} findings and were not trialled.

\paragraph{Feedback while the grade is still open.}
A comment can still change the model in front of the student, or it can change how a later model is made. S8's walk-around comments arrived while the project could still be changed. S10 still had time for a large local rewrite, and afterwards started asking who is external before the next BPMN. S10 said that a problem noticed only after delivery can teach the next piece of work, while that piece itself no longer changes. S7's grade came before the comments, so that delivery could not be resubmitted. The comments were for the next phase or the defence. After a later course had taught more notation, S8 went back over the bachelor's-project models and found small defects. The full redo in S7's group is not shown to have been caused by the locked grade alone. If a course has only one modelling submission, a comment during the lab may be the only chance to change that submission. It can still be of use on a later model. That distinction was not tested here.

\paragraph{A question at the desk.}
A public board helps students who will show an unfinished diagram, and it stops others. A question at the desk can recover work from a student who will not raise a hand and ask. Reserved students ask less often, so they can receive fewer comments than classmates who speak up. S10 found the desk easy and raising a hand hard. Access was also wider than shyness. Some students still treated an extra comment as something they had to ask for, and some shared with friends but not with strangers in the group. This was not tried as a change to a course.

\paragraph{Checks, not generators.}
PH3 and CAT13 point in the same direction for both modelling tools and AI: validation, explanations, and a tool that checks the syntax or refuses a model the language does not allow are closer to what participants asked for than a tool that draws the assignment. An experiment with a BPMN drawing tool found mixed results when extra feedback and points were added together \cite{garaccione2024gamification}. This thesis does not test whether a check would improve the model. L3's proposal belongs here rather than in CAT13: an LLM that explains the student's model back in ordinary language, not one that writes the diagram or the constraints. That reading is consistent with education-tailored feedback arguments in MDSE teaching \cite{liebel2017model} and should not be confused with a product requirement specification.

\paragraph{Teach the bridge between families.}
Where several notations are assessed, the difficulty was how the next language relates to the previous one (CAT14). S6 said the notations were known separately, but ``the bridge between them was unclear,'' and that nobody had explained ``how much of the iStar model should survive in UML'' (S6). S10 had tried to translate BPMN too directly into a class diagram, and was told that the models were answering different questions. S10 said that explanation unblocked the work. S8's requirements diagrams and class diagrams looked similar and still were not the same kind of model (CAT2). An implication is to teach that relation: what question each language answers, and where a direct copy is the wrong move. That teaching was not tested here. Reusing one case study across families is a wish reported in the interviews, not a tested curriculum.

\paragraph{How a new language relates to ones already known.}
A further implication is how a language just introduced relates to languages the students already know. \hyperref[app:l4t067]{L4} described models as complementary perspectives: each answers its own question, and together they give a better view of the problem. \hyperref[app:s6c14]{S6} asked for one continuous example from goals, through quality decisions, to UML, with the trace written down. An implication of those two accounts is to say, for each new language, what it shows on its own and what it shows only together with a language the students already know. That implication was not tested here.

\paragraph{Shared work and what the model was for.}
In the reported group episodes, what happened to the diagram went together with how the work was shared and what the model was for (PH4). That is not a rule that both are required. Peer review after split-by-notation can surface divergence; it does not by itself create a shared model (S10).

\section{Limits of the claims}
\label{sec:disc_limits}

Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology} stated how quality was pursued. This section states what those bounds do to the argument.

The study analyses what participants recounted. It did not watch them model, and it did not assess the diagrams. Recall is uneven. Some episodes were current courses. Others were years earlier, and S9's degree is recalled weakly. S9's remark about hiding a drawing is what they said they would do, not an event that happened. Some questions offered a word, including a compile button and whether notation was the biggest initial problem. A prompted answer remains the participant's account. Quotations are from cleaned working transcripts. For S7--S10 the English was drafted by an AI tool after identifying details were removed, and it was checked against the Portuguese. The recordings are gone. The thesis therefore does not say what a course taught, does not give one cause for S7's redo, and does not say that a diagram got better. S10 reported that a review made the diagrams explainable. That is not a measured improvement.

The participants were contacts, including course colleagues, and may differ from students who were not asked. The courses, degrees, and languages are not one setting. Claims about teaching time rest on four lecturer interviews from the first round. No lecturer and student from the same course were interviewed, so a lecturer's account of a room that waits is not checked against that room's students. That mismatch is one open question (OQ3). It is not the whole of these limits.

One person analysed the interviews and had studied modelling. That may have introduced biases that were not noticed while the interviews were being conducted. Comparing accounts does not remove that bias. Interviewing stopped because further interviews were hard to arrange and no 15th category appeared. That stop does not settle every relation inside the categories. Chapter~\ref{cha:findings} states which relations are claimed, and where a claim is thin.

The study did not test a change to a course or a tool. A wanted check, an explanation, or a review step is a reported need or a reported practice. It is not evidence that the change would improve learning. The implications in this chapter are proposals.

Student-side CAT1 and CAT4--CAT6, CAT9, and CAT12--CAT14 are denser after wave~2 than at the wave~1 freeze. No further lecturers were interviewed, so accounts of course load and of planned feedback that could not be kept still come from wave~1. CAT11 therefore remains partly the lecturers' view of institutional constraint, even though S7 and S8 added student load.

Wave~1 left five sampling questions (OQ1--OQ5). They are gaps, not extra categories. No lecturer and student from the same course offering were interviewed, which is why OQ3 is only partly answered: lecturers' ``students wait to start'' can describe the room without describing every student.

The stop is stated in Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}. After the four further interviews, the 14 categories were judged enough for the research questions of this thesis. Further details could still appear. No further lecturers were interviewed. The study does not claim that the categories were saturated. That stop is not, by itself, evidence that every relation inside the categories is settled.

Smaller leftover questions from individual memos (whether a checker is dropped only when it is optional; whether lab walk-around can replace a second submission; whether exclusive notation ownership was the teacher's design or a group habit) stay as future-work recipes, not as a recruitment plan for this thesis.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:disc_future}

A later study can take up the remaining questions without pretending that this master's project can still collect that sample. Table~\ref{tab:open_questions} already labels what wave~2 closed, what it only partly answered, and what it left untouched. The items below are recipes for a different project, not a recruitment plan for this thesis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Observe the first minutes of a board laboratory, or interview the lecturer of the same offering as the students, to test wait-to-start as a room-level versus person-level claim (OQ3 remainder).
    \item Ask, in a course that assesses more than one language, whether students were shown how the next language relates to the previous one before they had to move from one language to the other (OQ4 remainder).
    \item Collect non-leading incidents about choosing granularity and what to omit, or observe the same task, before treating an ``abstraction deficit'' as a student finding (OQ5).
    \item Compare other groups in the same systems-analysis setting on whether a full redo is common or was tied to one rushed teammate (S7), and whether exclusive notation ownership was the teacher's design or a group habit (S10).
\end{itemize}

Why interviewing stopped is stated in Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}. That judgment is not a claim that later studies of other countries, notations, or lecturer practice are unnecessary.
