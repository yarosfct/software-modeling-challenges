\chapter{Findings}
\label{cha:findings}

This chapter reports the grounded account that emerged from the 14 interviews. It is organised by research question rather than as a catalogue of fourteen disconnected labels. The analysis produced \textbf{14 categories} (CAT1--CAT14) and \textbf{four phenomena} (PH1--PH4). Phenomena are used as previously defined for this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:method_categories}). CAT2, CAT11, and CAT14 answer the research questions but do not feed a phenomenon: they contrast or condition other categories.

Quotations are short excerpts from the coded transcripts, identified by case. Portuguese interviews (S7--S10) are quoted from the English working transcripts used in analysis. Claims stay bounded by memo and case identifiers; student evidence is not merged with lecturer interpretation, and design wishes are not treated as tested interventions.

\section{RQ1: Challenges across modelling activities}
\label{sec:findings_rq1}

RQ1 asked what challenges students and instructors perceive, and how those challenges show up when people interpret, create, refine, and evaluate models. Four categories answer this question first: getting started (CAT1), too many symbols and frameworks (CAT2), judging whether a model is good enough (CAT12), and moving from one kind of diagram to another (CAT14). These are different problems. Waiting for a worked example is not the same as getting lost in arrows. A class diagram can look fine as UML and still drop the goals or the process that the previous model was meant to carry. Figure~\ref{fig:activity_matrix} places the four activities. Refining has no category of its own under RQ1: what can still be changed is CAT5, reported with the strategies in Section~\ref{sec:findings_rq2}.

\centering

Creating is the gated first diagram (CAT1) and, when the family changes, translation loss (CAT14). Evaluating is judging ``good enough'' when most of the tools the participants described did not compile the model (CAT12). A compile check would not show that the diagram said what was meant. Refining is dashed because it is CAT5, late feedback, and that category stays under RQ2.}

\subsection{CAT1 --- Software modeller's block}
\label{sec:cat1}

Getting from a written problem statement to the first real application was often gated on a demonstration, prior contact with the same notation, or some other worked path. Lecturers described a room that waits: ``They wait for the teacher to start the solution---probably cultural accommodation, though I am not sure'' (L1). Asked whether some students start anyway, the same lecturer added: ``Some do, but they are a minority. The others wait'' (L1). Students described the same gate from the other side when they had not yet seen the notation used on a live task.

Wave~2 added a different form of the same gate. In S8's bachelor's project, UML and requirements models were required when S8 did not know how to use those notations, even though earlier courses had already used entity-relationship and class diagrams: ``I had the idea that I had to use UML, requirements engineering, and so on, because it was in the project requirements, but I had no idea how to use those notations'' (S8). A later Software Engineering course introduced modelling more explicitly, but by then a homemade sketching routine was already in place (CAT4).

The gate is not universal. S3 could start the exercises immediately in a course on modelling systems and processes, because of earlier, even if shallow, BPMN contact: ``In MSP, I remember clearly that I could start the exercises as soon as the professor showed them. I did not need to wait'' (S3). S5 rarely waited for a teacher demonstration (``I rarely wait for a teacher demo''). S7 and S10 started without copying the board; wait-to-start can describe the \emph{room} (classmates who copy) without describing every student. S9, who recalled modelling at a distance, likewise needed to see the task done once before starting. Figure~\ref{fig:cat1} summarises those three forms of the gate.

\subsection{CAT2 --- Notation and symbol overload create doubt}
\label{sec:cat2}

Unfamiliar symbols, arrows, and frameworks created doubt at the start \emph{and} mid-work. This is distinct from CAT1: S1 could name the high-level task and still stall on notation. ``The high-level concepts were fairly straightforward, but the symbols and notation complicated things'' (S1). On a game-modelling project, ``Different arrows and boxes meant different things. There was a lot of information to absorb, and it felt arbitrary when trying to do something useful with it'' (S1). S2 located course difficulty in having to learn frameworks before drawing: ``I had to use frameworks with which I was unfamiliar. I had to learn them before I could start drawing'' (S2).

While switching notations, S8 found that ``requirements diagrams looked similar to class diagrams and had similar relations as well,'' and an example beside the task removed the confusion (S8). When S7 and S10 got stuck moving from one kind of diagram to another, that is discussed under CAT14 rather than as more symbol overload. Figure~\ref{fig:cat1_vs_cat2} separates the two mechanisms. A missing path and symbol overload are both ways the first move gets hard, and they can happen to the same student.

\subsection{CAT12 --- Hard to judge good enough}
\label{sec:cat12}

In this study, most models and modelling tools did not compile or simulate execution. Compiling a model would check that it is well-formed. It would not check that the diagram says what the person who made it meant. Students and lecturers described quality as a walkthrough, a justification, granularity, and readability, and more than one solution could be valid. They judged a model with checks of their own, such as explaining each element, matching the diagram to the written requirements, and readability.

S4 had no list from the instructor: ``I have a kind of personal checklist, although nobody gave it to us as an official rubric'' (S4). The three checks were whether every element could be explained in plain language, whether the diagram matched the written requirements, and whether it was readable enough for a supervisor. S7 treated practicals as gut feeling. On the exam the answer was not run, so S7 could not tell whether it was complete, and described the judgement as ``going on faith,'' after listing the requirements in ordinary language and crossing off the ones the diagram covered (S7). S10's group also had no list from the instructor. Their questions were whether the assignment was covered, whether one model contradicted another, and whether they could explain the diagram. Explainability was the defence test: ``If three people who made the model had difficulty explaining it, it was probably too complicated'' (S10).

L1 accepted more than one model---``in modelling there is not only one correct way'' (L1)---and still graded completeness, consistency, and readability. Those lists, and grading completeness, consistency, and readability, are how people decided the model was good enough. Nothing in the model ran and answered that question for them. A teacher's comment was the other way people learned whether the model was good enough. When that comment arrived (CAT5), and who could get it (CAT6), are reported later, with the claim that the everyday right-or-wrong signal was a person (PH2).

\subsection{CAT14 --- Languages answer different questions}
\label{sec:cat14}

The difficulty is a change of language, not only a change of symbols. These languages, and families of languages, serve different purposes. They express different concepts, and they are drawn differently. Trace from one to another is limited.

An iStar model and a class diagram are different views of a system. A class diagram is not a goal model, so the goals are not what should be looked for inside it. S6's hardest moment was not drawing actors but translating an iStar rationale model into UML: ``our pair produced a class diagram that looked syntactically correct but lost the goals'' (S6), and ``nobody had explicitly explained how much of the iStar model should survive in UML'' (S6). S6 also described the difference in stance: ``UML felt more structural. iStar felt organisational and political: who depends on whom, which goals conflict'' (S6).

S10 translated an external BPMN pool too directly into UML and was told afterwards that ``the models were answering different questions'' (S10). Names and requirements carried. Process structure did not become classes. S7's confusion at the switch was lighter, and concerned how the next language is drawn. S8's requirements diagrams and class diagrams are the similar-notation case reported under CAT2. These interviews do not show whether the courses had taught that difference before the diagram went wrong. Figure~\ref{fig:cat14} shows that limit.

\subsection{PH1 --- Turning a written problem statement into the first diagram}
\label{sec:ph1}

The hard step is making the first model from a written problem statement. Learning the symbols is a smaller difficulty. CAT1 (a missing path), CAT3 (examples that let the work start), and CAT4 (procedures students described for getting started) jointly support this phenomenon (Figure~\ref{fig:ph1}). CAT2 can delay the same first move. It is a different mechanism: too many symbols, where the path itself is not what is missing.

What PH1 is \emph{not}: a claim that every student waits, or that the symbols do not matter. S3, S5, S7, and S10 could start; the room can still wait. Teacher examples open the move from the problem statement to a first diagram (CAT3). CAT4 reports procedures some students used while making that move.

\section{RQ2: Strategies and support needs}
\label{sec:findings_rq2}

RQ2 asked what people do about the challenges and what pedagogical or tool support they identify. The primary categories are teacher examples (CAT3), student procedures for getting started (CAT4), late feedback (CAT5), access to feedback (CAT6), and AI used as an explainer (CAT13).

\subsection{CAT3 --- Teacher examples and walkthroughs unlock work}
\label{sec:cat3}

Worked examples and whiteboard walkthroughs were the main pedagogical unlock. S1 put it directly: ``Examples are more useful for me because I learn by seeing things'' (S1). L1 described a board ritual: map the statement into a model step by step, then give another exercise to do alone. Wave~2 instantiated the same pattern: S7 recalled a simple board example followed by class debate; S8 said examples taught notation more than the expected answer.

L4 used a different walkthrough. Students solved the exercise with the lecturer, who guided them, rather than the lecturer solving it for them first: ``No, I do not solve exercises for them. They solve the exercises with me'' (L4). That is another way of guiding the work. The other interviews still describe worked examples and board walkthroughs (S1, L1, S7, S8).

\subsection{CAT4 --- Procedures students used to get started}
\label{sec:cat4}

Students described procedures for getting from the problem to a first diagram: trial-and-error from the text, objects then relations, reading symbols as words, and early drafts. S5's group scripted the first ten minutes before opening a tool: ``Someone reads the text aloud, we each say what jumps out, and we decide who drafts the first diagram'' (S5).

Some of these procedures were part of what the class was doing. S7 started quickly ``to have something done in case I was the `victim' of the board'' (S7). Classmates who had not tried copied from the board. S10 did the exercise in a notebook and then compared it with what appeared on the board. S8 underlined requirements and moved from pencil to pen on a bachelor's project that required UML they did not yet know how to use; earlier courses had already used entity-relationship and class diagrams (CAT1). Table~\ref{tab:cat4_tactics} lists the procedures.

\subsection{CAT5 --- Feedback arrives too late}
\label{sec:cat5}

End-of-course or end-delivery feedback let errors compound. S1 wanted problems caught earlier: ``Problems could have been addressed along the way instead of propagating until the end'' (S1). Lecturers often planned several rounds of feedback, but class time and ECTS then cut those rounds short.

What students could still change depended on remaining time and on whether the grade was already locked. S8's individual project received walk-around comments that ``almost always implied changes, large or small'' (S8), with no remembered full redo. S10 still had time before the next delivery and did ``a large local restructuring'' (S10). S7 received the grade before comments---``The grade was given before the feedback'' (S7)---and a rushed teammate then forced a full rebuild: ``we had to completely redo the system from the first phase'' (S7). CAT9 (group tempo) interacts with CAT5 here. Who could get a comment while that window was open is CAT6.

\subsection{CAT6 --- Feedback access depends on initiative and format}
\label{sec:cat6}

Detailed feedback often arrived only if students asked, and public formats favoured those willing to expose a half-finished diagram. S4 preferred private channels: ``Not very comfortable in public. I am shy, so presenting a half-finished diagram to the whole class is stressful'' (S4). S5, more outgoing, used a public lab board. Wave~2 widened the conditions beyond a shy/outgoing split: S7 was comfortable showing \emph{models} (more than code) but still treated extra comments as asked-for and in person; S8 shared easily with friends and the teacher, not with course-group strangers; S9 would hide poor drawing rather than a wrong model; S10 found desk walk-around easy and raising a hand hard: ``If the teacher comes by your desk and asks `is everything all right?', it is much easier to show what you are doing than to raise your hand in front of the class'' (S10). For S8, practice exercises did not count toward the grade. The teacher did not check them, and going to ask was rare. S8 called that a lack of interest, not shyness, though S8 did go a few times. On the project, the teacher checked the work as it went, and those comments arrived while the work could still be changed (CAT5).

Figure~\ref{fig:cat5_cat6} keeps when a comment arrives (CAT5) and who can get it (CAT6) as two questions. They meet when the comment arrives while the model can still be changed.

\subsection{CAT13 --- AI used to explain or compare, not to generate graded models}
\label{sec:cat13}

Students who used AI asked it to explain options or compare approaches; they rejected generation for graded work. S5 used a chatbot ``to suggest alternative class diagrams from a bullet list, then compared options with the team'' and, for graded work, ``rewrote everything ourselves and documented changes'' rather than handing in the raw output (S5). S10's incident is the first Portuguese lived case in the corpus: screenshots and small questions after a self-check, never a full model---``I never asked `make me the model of this whole system'.'' (S10). That use also substituted for some public questions S10 avoided (CAT6). S4 and S6 reported the same stance, which later feeds PH3 (Section~\ref{sec:ph3}). Figure~\ref{fig:cat13} separates what they asked from what they would not hand in. One lecturer wanted an LLM that would explain a student's model back in ordinary language, so the student could compare that explanation with what they had meant---not a tool that draws the assignment (L3). That is a teaching idea, not a CAT13 finding, and it is not in the figure; Chapter~\ref{cha:discussion} returns to it.

\subsection{PH2 --- A review often comes from a person}
\label{sec:ph2}

In this study, most models and modelling tools did not compile or simulate execution (CAT12). Where a tool can compile a model, that check shows that the model is well-formed. It does not show that the model says what the person who made it meant, and it is not a review of the model. Students often learned whether a model was good enough from a person: a teacher's comment, including a late one (CAT5, CAT6). Whether a tool could check the model depended on the tool. Where a checker was available, wanting that check is PH3.

Walk-around, private messages, and public boards change who gets a teacher's comment in time (Figure~\ref{fig:ph2}). Students also reviewed models. L4 had groups work the same problem on the board, then swap and validate one another's work: ``It is a kind of peer review'' (L4). S4's own list, and S7's ``going on faith'' when an exam answer was not run, are checks students made themselves.

What PH2 is \emph{not}: a claim that automated checkers do not exist, that lecturers refuse to comment, or that students cannot review a model. Checkers appear under PH3 (Section~\ref{sec:ph3}). In these courses, a person was often the review that arrived.

\section{RQ3: Socio-technical conditions}
\label{sec:findings_rq3}

RQ3 asked how tools, course design, domains, and feedback practices shape modelling experience. The categories here are semantic checking (CAT7), tool friction (CAT8), groups (CAT9), real audiences (CAT10), and course load (CAT11). PH3 gathers the tool and AI support claim after CAT7 and CAT8; CAT13, reported under RQ2, feeds it as well. Course load cuts time for demonstrations and feedback.

\subsection{CAT7 --- Semantic checking is valued}
\label{sec:cat7}

Tools and checks that catch invalid models were wanted. This is not a claim that heavy tools always beat drawing. S3 valued a BPMN-specific interface: ``it presents a lot of the vocabulary directly in the interface'' (S3). Lecturers valued checkers such as piStar for iStar (L4) and preferred modelling tools over generic drawing when licensing allowed (L1).

Wave~2 added bounds. S7 saw an open-source compile tool presented and then dropped it: ``the interface was a bit antiquated and using it created more problems than it solved'' (S7). S8 skipped optional Camunda/Archi suites and used draw.io. Wanting checks (CAT7) and abandoning a checker for UI or optionality are compatible; they are not a new category.

\subsection{CAT8 --- Tool friction, switching, and cost}
\label{sec:cat8}

Option overload, licensing, mid-course switches, and slow or corrupt tools raised the cost of modelling. S1 found StarUML overwhelming for a simple relation: ``The tool had so many options that it became completely overwhelming to know how to make a simple entity-to-box relation'' (S1) and preferred a simpler drawing tool. Lecturers reported licensing pushing tool changes (L1). Wave~2 added an outdated compile interface that S7 then dropped, optional Camunda and Archi that S8 skipped in favour of draw.io, and UML menus S9 found outdated. Checks are still wanted (CAT7); a tool that is too complicated, optional, or slow will still be dropped. Figure~\ref{fig:cat7_cat8} keeps that wish separate from the cost. It does not include the request for explanations rather than a generated assignment, which is PH3.

\subsection{PH3 --- Want checks and explanations, not auto-drawn homework}
\label{sec:ph3}

PH2 is the everyday situation: the right/wrong signal is a person. PH3 is what participants asked for instead: validation and explanations, not a machine that draws the assignment (Figure~\ref{fig:ph3}). CAT7 (semantic checking valued), CAT8 (friction when tools are heavy), and CAT13 (AI as explainer) feed this phenomenon. CAT13 was reported under RQ2; it belongs here as a feeder, not as a second home for that category.

What PH3 is \emph{not}: evidence that a particular checker or chatbot works, or that lecturers' LLM redesign wishes (L3) are findings. Those wishes are implications (Chapter~\ref{cha:discussion}). CAT13 rests on four student cases, including one Portuguese incident (S10); it should not be over-read as an AI-education evaluation.

\subsection{CAT9 --- Groups can kill or save the model}
\label{sec:cat9}

Code-first pressure or passive teammates killed diagrams; ownership, pairs, live boards, and stand-ups kept them alive. S4's group wanted to jump to implementation on a generic shop domain while S4 was still drawing the class diagram. S8 is not used here: that project was individual, and showing work to friends rather than to course-group strangers is reported under CAT6. Wave~2: a teammate who rushed to finish phase~1 forced S7's pair into a from-scratch redo (CAT5 interaction). S10's group split the work by notation---one person did BPMN, another requirements, another UML---and the diagrams started to disagree: ``Then we had two versions of the same system'' (S10). They later added a draft-and-review step so everyone could still explain the models at the defence. Figure~\ref{fig:cat9} is that kill-or-save split; S9 is not used here.

\subsection{CAT10 --- Real audience makes modelling worth it}
\label{sec:cat10}

A concrete reader---supervisor, stakeholders, teaching assistant---pulled quality up more than domain excitement alone. S4 still held that ``Interesting domains make a big difference'' (S4), but wave~2 placed audience above that: course-only models ``would probably only pass through the teacher's hands'' (S7), whereas a thesis architecture for supervisor and defence became a priority. S10 similarly treated explainability as a defence criterion (CAT12). Domain interest matters; it does not replace a reader. Figure~\ref{fig:cat10} is that contrast. It is not the group split in CAT9, and it is not PH4.

\subsection{CAT11 --- Course load and theory--practice gaps}
\label{sec:cat11}

Heavy continuous assessment, missed theory, slide-only catch-up, and late recognition of modelling's value shaped what teaching could actually deliver. Lecturers described workload and continuous evaluation cutting attendance and time for demos and feedback (L1, L4). Wave~2 added a student dimension that is not the same as lecturer ECTS-scarcity: S7 tried to finish modelling quickly because a heavy semester left little interest---``because modelling was not something that interested me much, I tried to get those course tasks done as fast as possible so I could focus more on the others'' (S7). S8's paid tutoring job lowered interest more than other courses. When load is this high, there is less time for the demonstrations (CAT3) and the feedback rounds (CAT5) described earlier.

\subsection{PH4 --- A diagram survives only if someone owns it and someone reads it}
\label{sec:ph4}

Group ownership (CAT9) and a real audience (CAT10) jointly decide whether modelling stays alive and careful (Figure~\ref{fig:ph4}). A well-taught example (CAT3) does not keep a diagram alive in a group that has already switched to code. Course load (CAT11) is drawn as a blocker of the loops, not as a fourth feeder: when ECTS and concurrent work leave no time for demos or comments, ownership and audience have less to work with.

What PH4 is \emph{not}: a claim that interesting domains do not matter. S4 still held that they do. Domain excitement is a weaker pull than a supervisor, a defence panel, or another stakeholder who will actually read the diagram.

\section{Category summary}
\label{sec:findings_summary}

Table~\ref{tab:cat_rq_summary} lists each overview category with its research question, a one-line claim, and the memos that most strongly support it.
