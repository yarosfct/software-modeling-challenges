\chapter{Findings}
\label{cha:findings}

This chapter reports the grounded account that emerged from the 14 interviews. It is organised by research question rather than as a catalogue of fourteen disconnected labels. The analysis produced \textbf{14 categories} (CAT1--CAT14) and \textbf{four phenomena} (PH1--PH4). Phenomena are used as previously defined for this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:method_categories}). CAT2, CAT11, and CAT14 answer the research questions but do not feed a phenomenon: they contrast or condition other categories.

Quotations are short excerpts from those cleaned working transcripts, identified by case. They are not a playback of the recording. Portuguese interviews (S7--S10) are quoted from the English translation used in analysis. Claims stay bounded by memo and case identifiers; student evidence is not merged with lecturer interpretation, and design wishes are reported as support needs, not as tested interventions.

\section{RQ1: Challenges across modelling activities}
\label{sec:findings_rq1}

RQ1 asked what challenges students and instructors perceive, and how those challenges show up when people interpret, create, refine, and evaluate models. Four categories answer this question first: getting started (CAT1), unfamiliar marks, lookalike diagrams, and a lot to absorb (CAT2), judging whether a model is good enough (CAT12), and moving from one kind of diagram to another (CAT14). These are different problems. Waiting for a worked example is not the same as getting lost in arrows. A class diagram can look fine as UML and still leave out the goals or the process, which that diagram may not be for. Table~\ref{tab:activity_matrix} shows where those activities are discussed. A category can be relevant to more than one activity. CAT4 and CAT5 are written up under RQ2. That is a choice about where the category is discussed, not a claim that the activity happens only there.

\subsection{CAT1 --- Software modeller's block}
\label{sec:cat1}

Getting from a written problem statement to the first real application was often gated on a demonstration, prior contact with the same notation, or some other worked path. Lecturers described a room that waits: ``They wait for the teacher to start the solution---probably cultural accommodation, though I am not sure'' (L1). Asked whether some students start anyway, the same lecturer added: ``Some do, but they are a minority. The others wait'' (L1). Students described the same gate from the other side when they had not yet seen the notation used on a live task.

Wave~2 added a different form of the same gate. In S8's bachelor's project, UML and requirements models were required when S8 did not know how to use those notations, even though earlier courses had already used entity-relationship and class diagrams: ``I had the idea that I had to use UML, requirements engineering, and so on, because it was in the project requirements, but I had no idea how to use those notations'' (S8).

The gate is not universal. S3 could start the exercises immediately in a course on modelling systems and processes, because of earlier, even if shallow, BPMN contact: ``In MSP, I remember clearly that I could start the exercises as soon as the professor showed them. I did not need to wait'' (S3). S5 rarely waited for a teacher demonstration (``I rarely wait for a teacher demo''). S7 and S10 started without copying the board; wait-to-start can describe the \emph{room} (classmates who copy) without describing every student. S9, recalling company training in the United Kingdom at a distance, likewise needed to see the task done once before starting. Figure~\ref{fig:cat1} summarises those three forms of the gate.

\subsection{CAT2 --- Unfamiliar marks, lookalike diagrams, and a lot to absorb}
\label{sec:cat2}

Unfamiliar symbols, arrows, and frameworks created doubt at the start \emph{and} mid-work. This is distinct from CAT1: S1 could name the high-level task and still stall on notation. ``The high-level concepts were fairly straightforward, but the symbols and notation complicated things'' (S1). On a game-modelling project, ``Different arrows and boxes meant different things. There was a lot of information to absorb, and it felt arbitrary when trying to do something useful with it'' (S1). S2 located course difficulty in having to learn frameworks before drawing: ``I had to use frameworks with which I was unfamiliar. I had to learn them before I could start drawing'' (S2).

While switching notations, S8 found that ``requirements diagrams looked similar to class diagrams and had similar relations as well,'' and an example beside the task removed the confusion (S8). When S7 and S10 got stuck moving from one kind of diagram to another, that is discussed under CAT14 rather than as more of this notation difficulty. Figure~\ref{fig:cat1_vs_cat2} separates the two mechanisms. A missing path and this notation difficulty are both ways the first move gets hard, and they can happen to the same student.

\subsection{CAT12 --- Hard to judge good enough}
\label{sec:cat12}

In this study, models and modelling tools often did not compile or simulate execution. L3 did execute student models and used that to check whether they behaved correctly. Students and lecturers described quality as a walkthrough, a justification, granularity, and readability, and more than one solution could be valid. They judged a model with checks of their own, such as explaining each element, matching the diagram to the written requirements, and readability.

S4 had no list from the instructor: ``I have a kind of personal checklist, although nobody gave it to us as an official rubric'' (S4). The three checks were whether every element could be explained in plain language, whether the diagram matched the written requirements, and whether it was readable enough for a supervisor. S7 treated practicals as gut feeling. On the exam the answer was not run, so S7 could not tell whether it was complete, and described the judgement as ``going on faith,'' after listing the requirements in ordinary language and crossing off the ones the diagram covered (S7). S10's group also had no list from the instructor. Their questions were whether the assignment was covered, whether one model contradicted another, and whether they could explain the diagram. Explainability was the defence test: ``If three people who made the model had difficulty explaining it, it was probably too complicated'' (S10).

L1 accepted more than one model---``in modelling there is not only one correct way'' (L1)---and still graded completeness, consistency, and readability. Those lists, and grading completeness, consistency, and readability, are how people decided the model was good enough. Nothing in the model ran and answered that question for them. A teacher's comment was the other way people learned whether the model was good enough. When that comment arrived (CAT5), and who could get it (CAT6), are reported later, with the claim that the everyday right-or-wrong signal was a person (PH2).

\subsection{CAT14 --- Languages answer different questions}
\label{sec:cat14}

The difficulty is a change of language, not only a change of symbols. These languages, and families of languages, serve different purposes. They express different concepts, and they are drawn differently. Leaving something out of the next diagram can be the right move. A class diagram is not a goal model, so the goals are not what should be looked for inside it. That omission is not the same thing as a missing link between the two diagrams, and it is not the same thing as a student not knowing which parts should carry. S6's hardest moment was not drawing actors but translating an iStar rationale model into UML: ``our pair produced a class diagram that looked syntactically correct but lost the goals'' (S6), and ``nobody had explicitly explained how much of the iStar model should survive in UML'' (S6). S6 also described the difference in stance: ``UML felt more structural. iStar felt organisational and political: who depends on whom, which goals conflict'' (S6).

S10 translated an external BPMN pool too directly into UML and was told afterwards that ``the models were answering different questions'' (S10). Names and requirements carried. Process structure did not become classes. S10 said that explanation unblocked the work. S10 also remembered traceability being taught, especially from requirements to system elements, but not a rigorous trace across all the models. S7's confusion at the switch was lighter, and concerned how the next language is drawn. S8's requirements diagrams and class diagrams are the similar-notation case reported under CAT2. S6 said the relation had not been explained. S10 was told after the diagram had already gone wrong. The interviews do not show that the relation had been taught before the switch. Figure~\ref{fig:cat14} shows that limit.

\subsection{PH1 --- Turning a written problem statement into the first diagram}
\label{sec:ph1}

Making a first model from a written problem statement is one difficulty. CAT1, CAT3, and CAT4 support that part of the account (Figure~\ref{fig:ph1}). Difficulty with the notation is another. CAT2 can delay the same first move: the marks are unfamiliar, two diagrams look alike, or there is a lot to absorb. The path itself is not what is missing. The interviews do not rank one difficulty as smaller than the other. S3, asked whether notation was the biggest initial problem, said yes: ``the bigger initial problem was notation'' (S3). Other students described waiting for a worked path. Those are different accounts, and both can occur for the same student.

What PH1 is \emph{not}: a claim that every student waits, or that the symbols do not matter. S3, S5, S7, and S10 could start; the room can still wait. Teacher examples open the move from the problem statement to a first diagram (CAT3). CAT4 reports procedures some students used while making that move.

\section{RQ2: Strategies and support needs}
\label{sec:findings_rq2}

RQ2 asked what people do about the challenges and what pedagogical or tool support they identify. The primary categories are teacher examples (CAT3), student procedures for getting started (CAT4), when a comment can still be used (CAT5), access to feedback (CAT6), and AI used as an explainer (CAT13).

\subsection{CAT3 --- Teacher examples and walkthroughs unlock work}
\label{sec:cat3}

Worked examples and whiteboard walkthroughs were the main pedagogical unlock. S1 put it directly: ``Examples are more useful for me because I learn by seeing things'' (S1). L1 described a board ritual: map the statement into a model step by step, then give another exercise to do alone. Wave~2 instantiated the same pattern: S7 recalled a simple board example followed by class debate; S8 said examples taught notation more than the expected answer.

L4 used a different walkthrough. Students solved the exercise with the lecturer, who guided them, rather than the lecturer solving it for them first: ``No, I do not solve exercises for them. They solve the exercises with me'' (L4). That is another way of guiding the work. The other interviews still describe worked examples and board walkthroughs (S1, L1, S7, S8).

\subsection{CAT4 --- Procedures students used to get started}
\label{sec:cat4}

Students described procedures for getting from the problem to a first diagram: trial-and-error from the text, objects then relations, reading symbols as words, and early drafts. S5's group scripted the first ten minutes before opening a tool: ``Someone reads the text aloud, we each say what jumps out, and we decide who drafts the first diagram'' (S5).

Some of these procedures were part of what the class was doing. S7 started quickly ``to have something done in case I was the `victim' of the board'' (S7). Classmates who had not tried copied from the board. S10 did the exercise in a notebook and then compared it with what appeared on the board. S8 underlined requirements and moved from pencil to pen when describing later class exercises, and said they were already somewhat familiar with making models. That routine is not placed on the bachelor's project, where S8 did not know how to use the UML the requirements asked for; earlier courses had already used entity-relationship and class diagrams (CAT1). Table~\ref{tab:cat4_tactics} lists the procedures.

\subsection{CAT5 --- When a comment can still be used}
\label{sec:cat5}

End-of-course or end-delivery feedback let errors compound. S1 wanted problems caught earlier: ``Problems could have been addressed along the way instead of propagating until the end'' (S1). Lecturers often planned several rounds of feedback, but class time and ECTS then cut those rounds short.

S8's project received walk-around comments that ``almost always implied changes, large or small'' (S8), with no remembered full redo. Those comments arrived while the work could still be changed. S10 still had time before the next delivery and did ``a large local restructuring'' (S10). After that, S10 started asking who is external before drawing another BPMN. S8, after a later course, went back over the bachelor's-project models and found small defects in the syntax and the notations. S7's grade was given before the comments, and that delivery could not be resubmitted. The comments were for the next phase or the defence. The group then redid the first-phase system from scratch. S7 also described a teammate who rushed and did almost everything. The interviews do not show that the locked grade, rather than that rush, produced the redo. Who could get a comment while the work could still be changed is CAT6.

\subsection{CAT6 --- Feedback access depends on initiative and format}
\label{sec:cat6}

Detailed feedback often arrived only if students asked, and public formats favoured those willing to expose a half-finished diagram. S4 preferred private channels: ``Not very comfortable in public. I am shy, so presenting a half-finished diagram to the whole class is stressful'' (S4). S5, more outgoing, used a public lab board. Wave~2 widened the conditions beyond a shy/outgoing split: S7 was comfortable showing \emph{models} (more than code) but still treated extra comments as asked-for and in person; S8 shared easily with friends and the teacher, not with course-group strangers; S10 found desk walk-around easy and raising a hand hard: ``If the teacher comes by your desk and asks `is everything all right?', it is much easier to show what you are doing than to raise your hand in front of the class'' (S10). S9 said that finishing a model before the teacher solved it never happened. Asked what they would do, S9 said they would avoid showing it because the drawing would be poor, not because the model might be wrong. For S8, practice exercises did not count toward the grade. The teacher did not check them, and going to ask was rare. S8 called that a lack of interest, not shyness, though S8 did go a few times. On the project, the teacher checked the work as it went, and those comments arrived while the work could still be changed (CAT5).

Figure~\ref{fig:cat5_cat6} keeps when a comment arrives (CAT5) and who can get it (CAT6) as two questions. They meet when the comment arrives while the model can still be changed.

\subsection{CAT13 --- AI used to explain or compare}
\label{sec:cat13}

Students who used AI asked it to explain options or compare approaches. S5 also asked a chatbot ``to suggest alternative class diagrams from a bullet list, then compared options with the team'' and, for graded work, ``rewrote everything ourselves and documented changes'' rather than handing in the raw output (S5). S10's incident is the first Portuguese lived case in the corpus: screenshots and small questions after a self-check, never a full model---``I never asked `make me the model of this whole system'.'' (S10). That use also substituted for some public questions S10 avoided (CAT6). S4 and S6 reported the same stance, which later feeds PH3 (Section~\ref{sec:ph3}). Figure~\ref{fig:cat13} separates what they asked from what they would not hand in. L3 wanted an LLM that would explain a student's model back in ordinary language, so the student could compare that explanation with what they had meant, rather than a tool that draws the assignment (L3). That is a support need L3 identified. It is not evidence that such a tool works, and it is not one of the student uses in this category. It is not in the figure.

\subsection{PH2 --- A review often comes from a person}
\label{sec:ph2}

In this study, models and modelling tools often did not compile or simulate execution (CAT12). Students often learned whether a model was good enough from a person: a teacher's comment, including a late one (CAT5, CAT6). Whether a tool could check the model depended on the tool. Where a checker was available, wanting that check is PH3.

Walk-around, private messages, and public boards change who gets a teacher's comment in time (Figure~\ref{fig:ph2}). Students also reviewed models. L4 had groups work the same problem on the board, then swap and validate one another's work: ``It is a kind of peer review'' (L4). S4's own list, and S7's ``going on faith'' when an exam answer was not run, are checks students made themselves.

What PH2 is \emph{not}: a claim that automated checkers do not exist, that lecturers refuse to comment, or that students cannot review a model. Checkers appear under PH3 (Section~\ref{sec:ph3}). In these courses, a person was often the review that arrived.

\section{RQ3: Socio-technical conditions}
\label{sec:findings_rq3}

RQ3 asked how tools, course design, domains, and feedback practices shape modelling experience. The categories here are checks and guidance (CAT7), tool friction (CAT8), groups (CAT9), what the model is for (CAT10), and course load (CAT11). PH3 gathers the check and explanation claim from CAT7; CAT8 qualifies that claim and does not feed it. CAT13, reported under RQ2, feeds PH3 as well. Course load leaves less time for demonstrations and feedback. It does not feed PH4.

\subsection{CAT7 --- Checks and guidance are wanted}
\label{sec:cat7}

What was wanted was help with the notation, or a tool that will not accept a model the language does not allow. S3 valued a BPMN interface that ``presents a lot of the vocabulary directly in the interface,'' explains an object on hover, and can warn when a workflow is connected incorrectly (S3). S3 described that warning as syntax or grammar, not as a check that the diagram fits the situation. L4 valued a modelling tool that provided semantic checking. In L4's words, ``if a student builds a model in piStar, I know the model is semantically correct in terms of the language'' and ``if the student tries something against the language, the tool does not allow it'' (L4). That is L4's claim about the language rules. It is not a finding that the model said what the student meant. L1 preferred a modelling tool over generic drawing when the licence allowed. That is a preference for the kind of tool.

Wave~2 added bounds. S7 saw an open-source compile tool presented and then dropped it: ``the interface was a bit antiquated and using it created more problems than it solved'' (S7). S8 never used the optional modelling programs in that course, and used a general drawing program. What happened to the tool is reported under CAT8.

\subsection{CAT8 --- Tool friction, switching, and cost}
\label{sec:cat8}

Too many options, licensing, a change of tool during the course, or a large diagram that was slow and came back broken raised the cost of modelling. On a large diagram, S3 saved the file and later found one diagram on top of another, with the page broken. S1 found a modelling tool overwhelming for a simple relation: ``The tool had so many options that it became completely overwhelming to know how to make a simple entity-to-box relation'' (S1) and preferred a simpler drawing tool. That drawing tool does not check the model. A modelling tool has to present the language, so it cannot be as empty as a blank page. What these accounts add is that several modelling tools were still hard to use. Lecturers reported licensing pushing tool changes (L1). Wave~2 added three different outcomes. S7 tried an outdated compile interface and then dropped it. S8 never used the optional modelling programs in that course, and used a general drawing program. S9 found the UML menus outdated in that company training and kept the tool, because drawing by hand was worse and the tool offered descriptions and templates. Wanting a check (CAT7) did not decide what happened to the tool. Figure~\ref{fig:cat7_cat8} keeps that wish separate from the cost. It does not include the request for explanations rather than a generated assignment, which is PH3.

\subsection{PH3 --- Want checks and explanations, not auto-drawn homework}
\label{sec:ph3}

PH2 is the everyday situation: a person tells the student whether the model is right or wrong. PH3 is what participants asked for instead. They wanted a check and an explanation, not a tool that draws the assignment (Figure~\ref{fig:ph3}). CAT7 and CAT13 are part of this phenomenon. CAT8 is not. Wanting a check did not decide what happened to the tool. CAT13 is written up under RQ2. It is named again here because it is one of the accounts that make up PH3.

This is not evidence that a particular checker or chatbot works. L3 asked for a tool that would explain a student's model back. That is a support need L3 stated. It was not tested, and it is not one of the student uses in CAT13. CAT13 rests on four student cases. It is not an evaluation of AI in education.

\subsection{CAT9 --- What the group did with the diagram}
\label{sec:cat9}

S4's group wanted to jump to implementation on a generic shop domain while S4 was still drawing the class diagram. Refinement of that diagram stopped, and it no longer matched the code. A teammate who rushed to finish phase~1 left S7's group to rebuild the system from scratch. The grade had already been given, and that rushed teammate had done most of the first phase. The interviews do not separate those two. S10's group split the work by notation and the diagrams started to disagree: ``Then we had two versions of the same system'' (S10). They later added a draft-and-review step so everyone could still explain the models at the defence. S10 reported that the diagrams became explainable. The interviews do not include an assessment of whether the models were better. Figure~\ref{fig:cat9} shows those three outcomes.

\subsection{CAT10 --- What the model is for}
\label{sec:cat10}

S4 said that ``Interesting domains make a big difference'' (S4). S7 described course models that ``would probably only pass through the teacher's hands,'' and a thesis architecture for a supervisor and a defence that became a priority (S7). The teacher is already a reader. What S7 describes as different is the use: a course submission, or a model that had to explain a system. S10 treated being able to explain the diagram as a defence criterion (CAT12). These accounts do not rank domain interest against having a reader, and they do not show that either one raised the quality of the model. Figure~\ref{fig:cat10} shows those reports. It is not the group split in CAT9, and it is not PH4.

\subsection{CAT11 --- Course load and theory--practice gaps}
\label{sec:cat11}

Heavy continuous assessment, missed theory, slide-only catch-up, and late recognition of modelling's value shaped what teaching could actually deliver. Lecturers described workload and continuous evaluation cutting attendance and time for demos and feedback (L1, L4). Wave~2 added a student dimension that is not the same as lecturer ECTS-scarcity: S7 tried to finish modelling quickly because a heavy semester left little interest---``because modelling was not something that interested me much, I tried to get those course tasks done as fast as possible so I could focus more on the others'' (S7). S8's paid tutoring job lowered interest more than other courses. When load is this high, there is less time for the demonstrations (CAT3) and the feedback rounds (CAT5) described earlier.

\subsection{PH4 --- Shared work and what the model was for}
\label{sec:ph4}

In the group episodes, what happened to the diagram went together with how the work was shared and what the model was for. S4's group moved to implementation, and refinement of the class diagram stopped. S7's group rebuilt the system from scratch after a rushed phase. S10's group split the diagrams, then added a review so they could explain them at the defence. That is an association in those accounts. It is not a rule that a diagram is kept up only if someone owns it and someone reads it. A course model can still be handed in when the reader is the teacher. A worked example is not, in S4's account, what kept the group on the diagram after they had moved to code. Course load limits time for demonstrations and comments. It is not a third necessary condition. Figure~\ref{fig:ph4} shows how CAT9, CAT10, and CAT11 are drawn on that map.

What PH4 is \emph{not}: a claim that interesting domains do not matter. S4 still held that they do. The interviews do not rank that interest against who will read the model.

\section{Category summary}
\label{sec:findings_summary}

Table~\ref{tab:cat_rq_summary} lists each overview category with its research question, a one-line claim, and the memos that most strongly support it. Those identifiers point to the analytical notes reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:audit_trail}; they are not extra quotations. How categories feed PH1--PH4 is in Figures~\ref{fig:ph1}--\ref{fig:ph4}. Four relations are used in this chapter. Examples and a student's own procedure are ways a first diagram gets started. Difficulty with the notation is a different difficulty from a missing path, and both can occur for the same student. Wanting a check did not decide what happened to the tool. Course load leaves less time for demonstrations and comments. It does not block a loop. Other links that were drawn between categories are not claims of this thesis. This is not a finished global theory.
