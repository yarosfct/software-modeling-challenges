Software and conceptual modelling are commonly included in software engineering and related computing programmes. Students do not all treat those courses as equal to their programming courses. Existing research reports that students may describe modelling as a useful instrument or as extra documentation, and that evidence is dispersed across modelling traditions and study contexts, with limited integrative explanation of how challenges arise and how they relate to strategies, support, and course conditions.

This thesis investigates how software modelling is experienced, taught, and learned in higher education, treating modelling as a socio-technical activity. It addresses three research questions: (RQ1) what challenges students and instructors perceive and how they manifest across interpreting, creating, refining, and evaluating models; (RQ2) which strategies and pedagogical or tool-related support needs they identify; and (RQ3) how tools, course design, domains, and feedback practices shape those experiences.

The study used Socio-Technical Grounded Theory. Fourteen semi-structured interviews (four lecturers, ten students) were open-coded into 277 codes and 78 memos, then compared into 14 categories and four phenomena. A second round of four student interviews, recruited from available contacts who had done software modelling, added detail inside the 14 categories and did not add a 15th. At that point the 14 categories were judged enough for the research questions of this thesis. Further details could still appear. The study does not claim that the categories were saturated.

The account shows a gated first modelling move, distinct from difficulty with unfamiliar or lookalike notation, and difficulty judging whether a model is good enough when the modelling tools the participants described often did not compile or simulate the model. A comment could still change the open work, and who could get it depended on the channel. In one case the grade came before the comments, so that delivery could not be resubmitted. Participants wanted checks and explanations rather than a tool that draws the assignment. A group can stop refining a diagram, rebuild it, or add a review so the members can explain it. Domain interest and the use of the model, a course submission or a defence, were both reported and are not ranked. Heavy workloads leave less time for worked examples and for feedback while the model can still change. These patterns suggest possible changes to courses and tools. This study did not test whether those changes would help.
