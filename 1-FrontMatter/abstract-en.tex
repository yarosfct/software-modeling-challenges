Software and conceptual modelling are commonly included in software engineering and related computing programmes, but modelling is often a smaller part of the programme than programming, and students often treat it as less central than coding courses. Students frequently experience modelling as either an essential aid for understanding and communication or as burdensome extra documentation. Existing research offers valuable insights but is dispersed across modelling traditions and study contexts, with limited integrative explanation of how challenges arise and how they relate to strategies, support, and course conditions.

This thesis investigates how software modelling is experienced, taught, and learned in higher education, treating modelling as a socio-technical activity. It addresses three research questions: (RQ1) what challenges students and instructors perceive and how they manifest across interpreting, creating, refining, and evaluating models; (RQ2) which strategies and pedagogical or tool-related support needs they identify; and (RQ3) how tools, course design, domains, and feedback practices shape those experiences.

The study used Socio-Technical Grounded Theory. Fourteen semi-structured interviews (four lecturers, ten students) were open-coded into 277 codes and 78 memos, then compared into 14 categories and four phenomena. A second round of four student interviews, recruited from available contacts who had done software modelling, added detail inside the 14 categories and did not add a 15th. Those 14 categories were judged sufficient for the research questions of this thesis. Sims and Cilliers call that theoretical sufficiency. Further properties could still appear.

The account shows a gated first modelling move, distinct from symbol overload, and difficulty judging whether a model is good enough when most of the modelling tools the participants described did not compile or simulate the model. A compile check would show that a model is well-formed, and would leave open whether the diagram says what was meant. Feedback was often late or unequally accessible, and what could still be changed depended on remaining time and on whether the grade was already locked. Participants wanted checks and explanations rather than a tool that draws the assignment. A group can keep a shared diagram or abandon it, and people take more care when someone will read the model. Heavy workloads leave less time for worked examples and for feedback while the model can still change. These patterns suggest possible changes to courses and tools. This study did not test whether those changes would help.
