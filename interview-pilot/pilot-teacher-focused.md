# Teacher Interview Guide B - Focused High-Yield Version (20-30 min)

This version is designed to maximise thesis-relevant evidence in a short semi-structured interview. It is more direct, more incident-based, and less descriptive. It is the better choice when you want richer data rather than strong comparability with the earlier pilot.

## 0. Opening (30-45s)

**What you say**

> Thanks for meeting with me. I am studying how modelling is taught and learned in higher education, especially the difficulties students face, the kinds of support that help, and how teaching choices, tools, and course conditions shape that experience.
> With your permission, I would like to record so I do not miss details. This should take around 20 to 30 minutes. You can skip any question.

*(If not recording: "I will take notes instead.")*

---

## 1. Fast setup (1-2 min)

### Q1 - Minimal teaching context

**Ask**

* "To situate your answers, could you briefly tell me what modelling-related course or courses you teach, and which modelling families you cover?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Introductory or advanced?"
* "How long have you been teaching this content?"

---

## 2. What students should learn, and where the course puts the pressure (4-5 min)

### Q2 - Intended competence and assessment alignment

**Ask**

* "By the end of your course, what should students be able to do with modelling, and where in the course do you actually see whether they can do it?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Which activities matter most: interpreting, creating, refining, evaluating?"
* "What does a merely acceptable model look like, compared with a strong one?"
* "Is there anything you care about in modelling that your current assessment does not capture well?"

### Q3 - How students are expected to get from problem to model

**Ask**

* "When students face a new modelling task, what process do you expect them to follow from the problem statement to a first workable model?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do you teach that process explicitly?"
* "Where do they most often break down: getting started, abstraction, semantics, refinement, or quality checking?"
* "Are there misunderstandings that recur across different notations?"

---

## 3. Core evidence: concrete struggle and teacher interpretation (6-7 min)

### Q4 - Concrete struggle incident

**Ask**

* "Can you walk me through one concrete moment when students struggled with modelling in your course? I am interested in one real situation rather than a general list."

**Probe only if needed**

* "What was the task?"
* "What exactly were students stuck on?"
* "How did you recognise the problem?"
* "What conditions were shaping it: tool, time pressure, group work, unclear requirements, unfamiliar domain, or transition to a new modelling family?"
* "What does that situation tell you about what students find hard in modelling?"

### Q5 - Starting barrier and dependence on examples

**Ask**

* "In your experience, is the main difficulty understanding the notation, or getting started independently when there is no teacher demonstration in front of them?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do students wait for an example before they feel able to act?"
* "How do you decide when to demonstrate and when to step back?"
* "Do worked examples transfer well to new tasks, or do students still struggle once the surface details change?"

**Interviewer note**

* This question is intentionally sharper than in the comparable version because it tests a central hypothesis from the lecturer data.

---

## 4. Support, feedback, and quality judgement (6-7 min)

### Q6 - What support actually helps

**Ask**

* "When students hit those difficulties, what support seems to help them most, and what tends not to help much?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Worked examples, templates, checklists, peer discussion, critique sessions, tool support?"
* "Do you respond differently to starting problems, semantic problems, and quality-judgement problems?"
* "Have you tried any support that sounded promising but failed in practice?"

### Q7 - Feedback, revision, and model quality criteria

**Ask**

* "How do students learn what counts as a good model in your course: through explicit criteria, through feedback on submissions, or mostly by inference?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do you explicitly name criteria such as completeness, consistency, readability, or fit-for-purpose?"
* "Do students get chances to revise after feedback?"
* "Do your feedback practices help them self-critique later, or mainly correct the current assignment?"

---

## 5. Tools, transitions, and contextual constraints (4-5 min)

### Q8 - Tools as help or friction

**Ask**

* "Do your current tools mainly support modelling learning, or do they introduce extra friction?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Would you distinguish drawing tools from tools that actually teach or check modelling?"
* "What support would matter most: semantic checking, guidance on next steps, templates, comparison, collaboration?"
* "Have practical constraints such as cost, access, or usability shaped your tool choices?"

### Q9 - Course conditions that shape modelling learning

**Ask**

* "Beyond tools, what conditions most shape modelling learning in your course: workload, attendance, assignment domains, transitions between modelling families, something else?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "How much does domain familiarity matter?"
* "Do students struggle when moving from one modelling family to another?"
* "How much do attendance and workload limit what students can actually learn?"

---

## 6. Closing (2-3 min)

### Q10 - Most valuable realistic change

**Ask**

* "If you could make one realistic change that would most improve modelling learning in your context, what would it be?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Would that change target pedagogy, assessment, feedback, tools, or course conditions?"

### Q11 - Anything important missing

**Ask**

* "Is there anything important about teaching or learning modelling that I did not ask, but should have?"

**Close**

> Thank you. This was very helpful. If you are interested, I can share a short summary of findings later, and I may follow up with one or two clarifying questions.

---

## Why this version is different

* It reduces descriptive setup and moves faster to observable teaching practice.
* It privileges concrete incidents over general opinions.
* It directly tests themes that emerged from your current lecturer analysis: the starting barrier, dependence on worked examples, implicit quality criteria, transition between modelling families, tool support, and contextual constraints.

---

## Semi-structured guidance

* If the teacher gives a rich answer to Q4 or Q5, stay with it and cut later probes.
* Prioritise Q4, Q5, Q6, Q7, and Q9 if time becomes tight.
* Good opportunistic follow-up topics: why students wait for demonstrations, how teachers decide scaffolding fade-out, whether students can self-check quality, why certain tools were abandoned, and how workload or absenteeism changes what teachers can realistically expect.
* Keep the tone exploratory. The goal is not to confirm your current interpretation, but to let the teacher confirm, challenge, or nuance it.
