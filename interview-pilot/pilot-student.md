# Student Interview Pilot Guides (A / B / C)

This file holds all student interview variants for the software-modelling education study. Use the selection notes below to choose a variant, then run that section. Shared consent language and generic probes apply to every variant.

---

## 1. Purpose and when to use Variant A / B / C

Use this section to decide which student guide to take into the next pilot interview.

### Guide A — Comparable Revision

Choose this version when:

* you want continuity with the current student pilot
* you want broader coverage with cleaner wording and less overlap
* you expect the student to be reflective enough to answer both experience and course-design questions

Main characteristics:

* recognisable structure
* stronger incident-based questioning than the old version
* better coverage of worked examples, quality judgement, and workload without becoming too aggressive
* safer for cross-case comparison with earlier interviews

### Guide B — Focused High-Yield Version

Choose this version when:

* you want the most analytically useful data in a short interview
* you want to test the strongest themes from the student and lecturer analysis
* you want to foreground confidence, uncertainty, and dependence on demonstrations

Main characteristics:

* more direct and more diagnostic
* less time spent on broad opinions
* stronger focus on starting a model, worked example transfer, self-checking, sharing confidence, workload, and transitions between modelling families
* better if the goal is to refine the thesis argument quickly

### Guide C — Gap-Filling Version (current recommendation for new interviews)

Choose this version when:

* you are conducting interviews after the first coding cycle (S1-S3, L1-L4)
* you want to fill gaps: AI, group work, RE/stakeholders, cross-family transfer, large-course feedback, professional visibility
* you want worked examples of expected depth: see example-student-interview-s4.md (shy/individual), s5.md (group-positive), s6.md (RE/Munich)

Main characteristics:

* extends Guide B with Q8-Q14 gap blocks (use selectively)
* still incident-based and time-triage friendly
* does not recruit a "no prior exposure" stratum; prior exposure may still emerge in Q1/Q8

### Practical recommendation

For **new** interviews after the first analysis cycle, use **Guide C**. Use Guide B when you need a shorter diagnostic session without gap topics. Use Guide A when you want continuity with the earliest pilot structure.

### Shared interviewer reminders

* Do not try to ask every probe.
* Ask for one specific assignment, class, or exercise whenever the student becomes too abstract.
* If a strong theme emerges early, stay with it and cut later sections.
* Semi-structured does not mean unfocused: only follow a tangent if it clearly helps explain student difficulties, supports, or contextual conditions.

## 2. Shared consent, intro, and probes

### Goals

#### What do I want to understand?

- How people experience software/conceptual modelling (UML, BPMN, iStar, ER, etc)
- What they find hard / easy / valuable / pointless.
- How they think about learning outcomes / competencies
- Which teaching strategies, tools, domains, assignments help or hinder.
- Ideas / requirements for better support

#### Style

- Semi-structured: I should have a backbone of questions, but it is better to follow interesting threads.
- Open and non-judgmental: no right/wrong answers.
- Grounded-theory-friendly:
  - Start broad (like "tell me about ...")
  - Use probing ("can you give me an example?")
  - Stay as close as possible to the participants own words.

### Generic intro for both groups — 2/3 min

- Thank the participant for taking their time to do the interview.
- Give some context about the interview background, like, "im working on a thesis about ...,"
- Explain why you picked that person for the interview : "im interested in your experience with ..., what works, what doesnt, what would help with ..."
- Then inform the participant about the expected/approcimated duration of the interview, inform that you will be taking notes or even recording audio, therefore getting the interview consent is priority ("if you are okay with, ill be ...").
- Assure the interviewer that his/her identity will remain anonymised, " i wont use your name or anything that directly identifies you in the thesis".
- Reasure the interviewer that there are no right or wrong answer and what im really looking for is honest opinions, that the interviewer is allowed and encouraged to make questions if anything is unclear, and if by chance there is some question that the interviewer doesnt want to answer, he can just say no.
- Lastly ask the interviewer if he got any questions before starting, and after that, ask permission for recording and start the interview.

### Follow-up prompts that can be useful

Whenever someone says something intersting, this can be used as generic probes:

- "Can you tell me a bit more about that?"
- "What do you mean by [their word] in this context?"
- "Can you give me a concrete example?"
- "How did you feel in that situation?"
- "What made that particularly easy/hard/frustrating...?"
- "How did you deal with that at the time?"
- "If you could change something about that, what would it be?"
- "Has this happened more than once? When else?"
- ... I'll add more later

## 3. Variant A — Comparable Revision (20-30 min)

This version keeps continuity with the current student pilot, but trims abstract questions, reduces overlap, and puts more weight on concrete learning experiences. It is still semi-structured: ask the core questions, use only the probes that help the student become specific, and follow emerging topics when they clearly matter for the thesis.

### 0. Opening (30-45s)

**What you say**

> Thanks for meeting with me. I am studying how modelling is taught and learned in higher education, especially the difficulties students face, what helps them learn, and how things like feedback, tools, assignments, and workload shape that experience.
> With your permission, I would like to record so I do not miss details. This should take around 20 to 30 minutes. You can skip any question.

*(If not recording: "I will take notes instead.")*

---

### 1. Background and context (2-3 min)

#### Q1 - Where modelling fits in your studies

**Ask**

* "Can you briefly tell me about your studies and where modelling fits into them?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Which courses have included modelling so far?"
* "What kinds of diagrams or notations have you used?"
* "Have you ever used models outside coursework, for example in a project, internship, or personal work?"

---

### 2. Experience with modelling (5-6 min)

#### Q2 - What modelling feels like to you

**Ask**

* "When you think about software modelling or conceptual modelling, do you see it mostly as useful, frustrating, extra documentation, or something else?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "What makes you see it that way?"
* "Has that view changed over time?"

#### Q3 - A moment when modelling felt useful

**Ask**

* "Can you remember a moment when modelling felt genuinely useful to you?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "What was the context?"
* "What exactly did the modelling help you do or understand?"
* "Did it change how you saw the system or problem?"

#### Q4 - A moment when modelling felt confusing or frustrating

**Ask**

* "Can you remember a moment when modelling felt confusing, pointless, or frustrating?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "What made it difficult: the notation, the tool, the task, the instructions, the group, or something else?"
* "What did you do when that happened?"
* "What would have helped you handle that situation better?"

**Interviewer note**

* Q3 and Q4 are high-yield. If the student answers in a general way, ask for one concrete assignment, class, or project.

---

### 3. Learning process and challenges (8-9 min)

#### Q5 - Hardest part when learning a new notation or framework

**Ask**

* "When you learn a new modelling notation or framework, what is usually the hardest part at the beginning?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Is it more about remembering symbols, understanding concepts, or knowing how to start from a text or problem?"
* "Was there a specific notation or framework where this became very obvious?"

#### Q6 - How you go from problem description to model

**Ask**

* "Imagine you get a text description of a system and need to build a model. What steps do you personally take?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Where do you usually start, and why?"
* "Does it feel like a structured process or more like trial and error?"
* "What helps you decide what to include and what to leave out?"
* "Has any teacher ever explicitly taught you a step-by-step approach for doing this?"

#### Q7 - Worked examples and starting confidence

**Ask**

* "When a teacher shows a worked example or solves a modelling exercise step by step, how much does that help when you later have to solve a similar task on your own?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do you feel able to transfer that example, or do you usually need another example first?"
* "If there is no example yet, how confident do you feel starting on your own?"
* "Do you tend to wait for the teacher's demonstration before you feel comfortable moving ahead?"

#### Q8 - How you judge whether a model is good enough

**Ask**

* "Once you have a model, how do you decide whether it is good enough or needs changes?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do you use any mental criteria such as completeness, consistency, readability, or fit to the requirements?"
* "Have you ever been given quality rules, a checklist, or a rubric?"
* "If multiple solutions could be valid, how do you handle the uncertainty of whether your model is acceptable?"

---

### 4. Tools, assignments, and course conditions (5-6 min)

#### Q9 - Tools and what they change

**Ask**

* "What tools have you used for modelling, and how has that been for you?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Any tool you particularly liked or disliked? Why?"
* "Do tools ever make modelling feel harder or easier than it actually is?"
* "Would guidance or suggestions from a tool help you, or would that be annoying?"

#### Q10 - Assignments, domains, and course pressure

**Ask**

* "Thinking about modelling assignments or projects, what kinds of domains have you worked with, and what course conditions made modelling easier or harder for you?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Were any domains especially motivating or especially hard to relate to?"
* "Have you ever been able to choose your own domain?"
* "Did workload, deadlines, or missing classes affect how well you could learn modelling?"
* "Did transitions between different modelling families ever feel difficult, or not really?"

---

### 5. Closing (3-4 min)

#### Q11 - What you would change

**Ask**

* "If you could change one or two things about how modelling is taught in your degree, what would you change?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Anything about explanations or examples?"
* "Anything about tools?"
* "Anything about assignments, workload, or feedback?"

#### Q12 - Anything missing

**Ask**

* "Is there anything we have not talked about that you think is important about learning or using modelling?"

**Close**

> Thank you. This was very helpful. If needed, may I contact you later for a short clarification?

---

### Quick coverage

* Challenges and sticking points -> Q4, Q5, Q6, Q7, Q8
* Supports and coping strategies -> Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9, Q11
* Contextual and course factors -> Q1, Q4, Q9, Q10

---

### Semi-structured guidance

* Prioritise Q4, Q6, Q7, and Q8 if time becomes tight.
* Ask for a concrete example whenever the student gives a broad opinion.
* Good follow-up topics if they arise naturally: waiting for teacher demonstrations, confidence in sharing a solution before the teacher solves it, relation semantics, workload pressure, and differences between formal coursework modelling and informal modelling outside class.
* Do not try to ask every probe. The goal is depth on the most diagnostic experiences.

## 4. Variant B — Focused High-Yield Version (20-30 min)

This version is designed to produce richer evidence in less time. It is more direct, more incident-based, and more tightly aligned with the strongest themes from your student analysis: starting modelling, dependence on demonstrations, transfer from worked examples, quality self-checking, confidence in sharing, and course pressure.

### 0. Opening (30-45s)

**What you say**

> Thanks for meeting with me. I am studying how students learn modelling in higher education, especially what feels difficult, what helps, and how teaching choices, tools, and course conditions affect that experience.
> With your permission, I would like to record so I do not miss details. This should take around 20 to 30 minutes. You can skip any question.

*(If not recording: "I will take notes instead.")*

---

### 1. Fast setup (1-2 min)

#### Q1 - Minimal context

**Ask**

* "To situate your answers, could you briefly tell me your programme, year, and which modelling-related courses or notations you have worked with?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Have you also used models outside class?"

---

### 2. Core learning experience (6-7 min)

#### Q2 - Concrete frustrating moment

**Ask**

* "Can you walk me through one concrete moment when modelling felt difficult, confusing, or frustrating? I am interested in one real situation rather than a general opinion."

**Probe only if needed**

* "What was the task?"
* "What exactly made it difficult?"
* "Was the problem the notation, the framework, deciding how to start, the tool, the assignment, the teacher explanation, or something else?"
* "What did you do next?"

#### Q3 - Starting a model and acting without a demo

**Ask**

* "When you have to build a model from a new problem, what is your first move?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do you feel you have a process, or is it mostly trial and error?"
* "If the teacher has not shown an example yet, how confident do you feel starting on your own?"
* "Do you tend to wait for a demonstration before you feel comfortable proceeding?"

#### Q4 - What teacher examples actually do for you

**Ask**

* "When a teacher solves an example on the board or shows a worked model, what does that actually help you with?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Does it help you understand the notation, the reasoning process, or just what the expected answer looks like?"
* "Can you usually transfer that example to a similar task later, or do you need another example first?"
* "Have there been times when an example looked clear in class but did not help much once you were alone?"

---

### 3. Quality, uncertainty, and sharing (6-7 min)

#### Q5 - How you judge your own model

**Ask**

* "When you finish a model, how do you decide whether it is good enough?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "What do you check for?"
* "Were you ever taught explicit quality criteria, a checklist, or a rubric?"
* "Do you compare your model with examples, classmates, or previous solutions?"

#### Q6 - Multiple valid answers and uncertainty

**Ask**

* "Have you ever finished a model and still felt unsure because more than one answer might be valid?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "How did you handle that uncertainty?"
* "Did you rely on teacher feedback, classmates, or your own judgement?"
* "Did that uncertainty make you less willing to share your solution?"

#### Q7 - Confidence in sharing before validation

**Ask**

* "If you complete a model before the teacher shows a solution, how comfortable do you feel sharing your approach?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Would you show it to classmates? To the teacher? To the whole class?"
* "What usually makes you hesitate?"
* "What would make you confident enough to share earlier?"

---

### 4. Tools, course design, and contextual constraints (5-6 min)

#### Q8 - Tools as help or friction

**Ask**

* "How have modelling tools affected your experience?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Which tools felt easier to work with, and which felt more frustrating?"
* "Was the hard part drawing, choosing relations, organizing the layout, or something else?"
* "Would guidance from a tool, such as suggestions or warnings, help you?"

#### Q9 - Workload, course design, and transitions

**Ask**

* "How much do course conditions affect your ability to learn modelling properly?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do modelling tasks feel rushed because of workload or competing deadlines?"
* "Have you skipped classes or practice because of pressure from other courses?"
* "Were the class materials enough when you missed something, or did you depend on the teacher's live explanation?"
* "When you switched between different modelling families or notations, did that feel natural or like starting over?"

#### Q10 - When modelling feels meaningful

**Ask**

* "What kinds of assignments or domains make modelling feel meaningful or worth the effort for you?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Are there domains that make you more engaged?"
* "Have you ever felt more comfortable modelling outside formal coursework than inside it?"
* "If you could choose more of the project context, would that change how you approach modelling?"

---

### 5. Closing (2-3 min)

#### Q11 - Most useful teaching change

**Ask**

* "If you could change one realistic thing about how modelling is taught, what would help you most?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Would that be more examples, more explicit steps, better feedback, different tools, more time, or something else?"

#### Q12 - Anything important missing

**Ask**

* "Is there anything important about learning modelling that I did not ask, but should have?"

**Close**

> Thank you. This was very helpful. If needed, may I contact you later for a short clarification?

---

### Why this version is different

* It moves quickly to concrete moments instead of broad opinions.
* It directly tests the strongest current hypotheses from your notes and student coding.
* It makes room for student confidence, uncertainty, and dependence on teacher demonstrations, which were underdeveloped in the original guide.

---

### Semi-structured guidance

* If the student gives a rich answer to Q2, Q3, or Q4, stay there and trim later probes.
* Prioritise Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5, Q7, and Q9 if time becomes tight.
* Good opportunistic follow-up topics: relation semantics, differences between formal and informal modelling, group work effects, and whether students actually use feedback to self-correct later.
* Keep the tone exploratory. The goal is to understand how the student experiences modelling, not to push them toward your current interpretation.

## 5. Variant C — Gap-Filling Version (25–30 min)

This version extends Guide B (Variant B above) with probes aligned to under-covered themes in the current corpus: cross-family transfer, requirements/stakeholder work, group modelling, AI use, large-course feedback, executable models, professional visibility, and audience-aware quality.

**Use when:** you want interviews that add analytic depth on RQ2/RQ3 gaps, not only repeat core challenge themes (starting, notation, tools, feedback).

**Do not use when:** you need strict comparability with Guide A/B pilots from earlier in the study.

---

### 0. Opening (30–45s)

**What you say**

> Thanks for meeting with me. I am studying how students learn modelling in higher education, especially what feels difficult, what helps, and how teaching choices, tools, and course conditions affect that experience.
> With your permission, I would like to record so I do not miss details. This should take around 25 to 30 minutes. You can skip any question.

*(If not recording: "I will take notes instead.")*

---

### 1. Fast setup (1–2 min)

#### Q1 — Minimal context

**Ask**

* "To situate your answers, could you briefly tell me your programme, year, and which modelling-related courses or notations you have worked with?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Have you used models outside class — for example in a thesis, internship, or job?"
* "Did you meet the same notation in more than one course, or several different families like UML, BPMN, iStar, and so on?"

---

### 2. Core learning experience (5–6 min)

#### Q2 — Concrete frustrating moment

**Ask**

* "Can you walk me through one concrete moment when modelling felt difficult, confusing, or frustrating? I am interested in one real situation rather than a general opinion."

**Probe only if needed**

* "What was the task?"
* "What exactly made it difficult?"
* "Was the problem the notation, the framework, deciding how to start, the tool, the assignment, the teacher explanation, the domain, or something else?"
* "What did you do next?"

#### Q3 — First steps on a new problem

**Ask**

* "When you get a text description and have to build a model, what happens in the first ten minutes? What is your first move?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Do you feel you have a process, or is it mostly trial and error?"
* "If the teacher has not shown an example yet, how confident do you feel starting on your own?"
* "Do you tend to wait for a demonstration before you feel comfortable proceeding?"

#### Q4 — What teacher examples actually do for you

**Ask**

* "When a teacher solves an example on the board or shows a worked model, what does that actually help you with?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Does it help you understand the notation, the reasoning process, or just what the expected answer looks like?"
* "Can you usually transfer that example to a similar task later, or do you need another example first?"
* "Have there been times when an example looked clear in class but did not help much once you were alone?"

### 3. Quality, uncertainty, and sharing (5–6 min)

#### Q5 — How you judge your own model

**Ask**

* "When you finish a model, how do you decide whether it is good enough?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "What do you check for — completeness, consistency, readability, fit to the problem?"
* "Were you ever taught explicit quality criteria, a checklist, or a rubric?"
* "Do you compare your model with examples, classmates, or previous solutions?"
* "Can you read your model back and recover what the original problem asked for?"

#### Q6 — Multiple valid answers and uncertainty

**Ask**

* "Have you ever finished a model and still felt unsure because more than one answer might be valid?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "How did you handle that uncertainty?"
* "Did you rely on teacher feedback, classmates, or your own judgement?"
* "Did that uncertainty make you less willing to share your solution?"

#### Q7 — Confidence in sharing before validation

**Ask**

* "If you complete a model before the teacher shows a solution, how comfortable do you feel sharing your approach?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Would you show it to classmates? To the teacher? To the whole class?"
* "What usually makes you hesitate?"
* "What would make you confident enough to share earlier?"

---

### 4. Gap topics — use selectively (8–9 min)

*Skip blocks that do not apply. Prioritise blocks that match the participant's background.*

#### Q8 — Cross-family and repeated exposure

*(Use if the student has taken more than one modelling course or notation.)*

**Ask**

* "If you learned more than one modelling notation or family, what carried over and what felt like starting over?"
* "Did meeting a notation earlier in another course change how you experienced a later course?"

#### Q9 — Requirements, stakeholders, justification

*(Use for RE, goal-oriented, iStar, or courses with vague briefs.)*

**Ask**

* "Have you had assignments with intentionally vague or incomplete problem statements?"
* "Did you interview stakeholders, do role-play, or have to justify why your model was built that way?"
* "Was it harder to move from goal-oriented or requirements models to something more concrete like UML?"

#### Q10 — Group and collaborative modelling

**Ask**

* "In group projects, who actually built and owned the model?"
* "Did modelling help the team share a picture of the system, or did it become overhead?"
* "Have you reviewed classmates' models — what made that useful or superficial?"

#### Q11 — AI and modelling

**Ask**

* "Have you used tools like ChatGPT, Copilot, or similar to create, explain, or check a model or diagram?"
* "Was that helpful feedback, or more of a shortcut?"
* "Would you want a tool that explains what your model means in plain language?"
* "Has AI changed how you prepare for projects or exams involving modelling?"

#### Q12 — Tools, scale, and course conditions

**Ask**

* "How have modelling tools affected your experience?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Which tools felt easier to work with, and which felt more frustrating?"
* "Was the hard part drawing, choosing relations, organising layout, or something else?"
* **Large course:** "In a large cohort, what feedback did you actually get on models — lecturer, TA, forum, automated checking?"
* "If you missed classes, were slides and materials enough, or did you depend on live explanation?"
* **If applicable:** "Did you run or simulate models — for example executable UML? Did that help learning or add complexity?"

---

### 5. Meaning and professional context (3–4 min)

#### Q13 — Domains and audience

**Ask**

* "What kinds of assignments or domains make modelling feel meaningful or worth the effort for you?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Are there domains that make you more engaged, or others that drain your motivation?"
* "Have you modelled for someone who would analyse or use the diagram — a supervisor, client, or experimenter? Did that change how carefully you built it?"

#### Q14 — Outside university

**Ask**

* "Have you used modelling in an internship, job, or thesis beyond formal coursework? Which notations, and was anything surprising compared to university?"

---

### 6. Closing (2–3 min)

#### Q15 — Most useful teaching change

**Ask**

* "If you could change one realistic thing about how modelling is taught, what would help you most?"

**Probe only if needed**

* "Would that be more examples, more explicit steps, better feedback, different tools, more time, theory-practice alignment, or something else?"

#### Q16 — Anything important missing

**Ask**

* "Is there anything important about learning modelling that I did not ask, but should have?"

**Close**

> Thank you. This was very helpful. If needed, may I contact you later for a short clarification?

---

### Semi-structured guidance

#### Time triage (if running short)

**Always prioritise:** Q2, Q3, Q5, Q7, Q11.

**Drop first:** Q14, executable-model probe in Q12.

**Context-specific priority:**

| Participant profile | Emphasise |
|-------------------|-----------|
| RE / goal-oriented course | Q9 |
| Large cohort course | Q12 feedback probes |
| Multiple notations | Q8 |
| Active AI user | Q11 |
| Thesis / internship modelling | Q14 |

#### Opportunistic follow-ups

* Relation semantics and cardinality confusion
* Differences between informal sketches and formal coursework models
* Whether feedback changed later self-checking habits
* Layout and readability for someone else reading the model

#### Inclusion (gentle, optional)

Only if the participant raises it or it clearly fits: shyness with whiteboard or public sharing; time pressure in exams; difficulty with dense visual diagrams.

#### Interviewer discipline

* Ask for one specific assignment, class, or exercise when answers become abstract.
* Semi-structured does not mean unfocused: follow tangents only when they explain difficulties, supports, or contextual conditions.
* Do not try to ask every probe.

## 6. Appendix — Guia B em pt-PT (versão focada)

# Guia de Entrevista a Estudantes B - Versão Focada de Alto Rendimento (20-30 min)

Esta versão foi pensada para produzir evidência mais rica em menos tempo. É mais direta, mais centrada em incidentes concretos, e mais alinhada com os temas mais fortes da tua análise dos estudantes: começar a modelar, dependência de demonstrações, transferência a partir de exemplos resolvidos, autoavaliação da qualidade, confiança para partilhar, e pressão da unidade curricular.

### 0. Abertura (30-45s)

**O que dizer**

> Obrigado/a por aceitares falar comigo. Estou a estudar como os estudantes aprendem modelação no ensino superior, especialmente o que sentem como difícil, o que ajuda, e como as escolhas de ensino, as ferramentas e as condições da unidade curricular afetam essa experiência.
> Com a tua permissão, gostava de gravar a entrevista para não perder detalhes. Deve demorar cerca de 20 a 30 minutos. Podes não responder a qualquer pergunta.

*(Se não for gravado: "Nesse caso, vou tomar notas.")*

---

### 1. Contexto rápido (1-2 min)

#### Q1 - Contexto mínimo

**Perguntar**

* "Para situar as tuas respostas, podes dizer-me brevemente o teu curso, ano, e que unidades curriculares ou notações relacionadas com modelação já usaste?"

**Explorar apenas se necessário**

* "Também já usaste modelos fora das aulas?"

---

### 2. Experiência central de aprendizagem (6-7 min)

#### Q2 - Momento concreto de frustração

**Perguntar**

* "Podes descrever-me um momento concreto em que a modelação te pareceu difícil, confusa ou frustrante? Interessa-me uma situação real, mais do que uma opinião geral."

**Explorar apenas se necessário**

* "Qual era a tarefa?"
* "O que é que, exatamente, a tornou difícil?"
* "O problema estava na notação, no enquadramento, em decidir como começar, na ferramenta, no enunciado, na explicação do docente, ou noutra coisa?"
* "O que fizeste a seguir?"

#### Q3 - Começar um modelo e avançar sem demonstração

**Perguntar**

* "Quando tens de construir um modelo a partir de um problema novo, qual é o teu primeiro passo?"

**Explorar apenas se necessário**

* "Sentes que tens um processo, ou é sobretudo tentativa e erro?"
* "Se o docente ainda não mostrou um exemplo, até que ponto te sentes confiante para começar sozinho/a?"
* "Tens tendência a esperar por uma demonstração antes de te sentires confortável para avançar?"

#### Q4 - O que os exemplos do docente fazem por ti

**Perguntar**

* "Quando um docente resolve um exemplo no quadro ou mostra um modelo resolvido, em que é que isso te ajuda concretamente?"

**Explorar apenas se necessário**

* "Ajuda-te a compreender a notação, o processo de raciocínio, ou apenas o aspeto que a resposta esperada deve ter?"
* "Normalmente consegues transferir esse exemplo para uma tarefa parecida mais tarde, ou precisas primeiro de outro exemplo?"
* "Já houve situações em que um exemplo parecia claro na aula, mas depois não ajudou muito quando estavas sozinho/a?"

#### Q6 - Várias respostas válidas e incerteza

**Perguntar**

**Explorar apenas se necessário**

* "Essa incerteza fez-te ter menos vontade de partilhar a tua solução?"

#### Q7 - Confiança para partilhar antes da validação

* "O que te faria sentir confiante para partilhar mais cedo?"

### Porque é que esta versão é diferente

* Avança rapidamente para momentos concretos em vez de opiniões gerais.
* Testa diretamente as hipóteses atuais mais fortes das tuas notas e da codificação dos estudantes.
* Dá espaço à confiança dos estudantes, à incerteza e à dependência de demonstrações do docente, aspetos que estavam menos desenvolvidos no guia original.

---

### Orientação semiestruturada

* Se o estudante der uma resposta rica a Q2, Q3 ou Q4, fica nesse ponto e corta explorações posteriores.
* Dá prioridade a Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5, Q7 e Q9 se o tempo ficar apertado.
* Bons tópicos de seguimento oportunista: semântica das relações, diferenças entre modelação formal e informal, efeitos do trabalho de grupo, e se os estudantes usam realmente o feedback para se autocorrigirem mais tarde.
* Mantém um tom exploratório. O objetivo é compreender como o estudante experiencia a modelação, não conduzi-lo para a tua interpretação atual.
